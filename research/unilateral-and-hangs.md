# Unilateral training and hangs, for a perceived shoulder/lat asymmetry — Research Findings

Commissioned question: should the program add (a) more unilateral exercises and
(b) dead/active hangs, to address a felt but unmeasured shoulder-girdle/lat
asymmetry. I am not a clinician. Nothing here establishes that the athlete
has a real asymmetry — only `research/physique-metrics.md` (no validated
photo protocol, 2–19.5% side difference is the documented norm by joint) and
`research/pressing-asymmetry.md` (same question, OHP, same conclusion: plausible,
unmeasured, no trial anywhere shows correcting a sub-clinical asymmetry improves
anything) exist as prior project findings, and this file inherits their
standard rather than relitigating it.

**Access note.** Several claims that circulated in general web search about
dead hangs — a specific "Gill 2016, *Spine Journal*, 30% intradiscal-pressure
reduction" citation and a specific "Inoue 2019, *Journal of Physical Therapy
Science*, 8.3° shoulder-flexion gain" citation — could not be traced to a real
indexed paper after a direct search for the author, year and journal. Real
disc-pressure-under-traction literature exists (Wilke 1999 and others, not
about hanging specifically), but these two specific numbers are flagged as
**unverifiable, likely a search-summarizer fabrication, and not used below.**
This is exactly the failure mode the brief warns about, and the house rule in
this project (`physique-metrics.md`, `pressing-asymmetry.md`) is to say so
rather than launder it into a citation.

## Summary

- **No programme change is justified by what exists today.** There is no
  measured asymmetry (two unmeasured visual impressions, same confound
  `physique-metrics.md` already flagged for photos, now extended to a video
  and a feeling), and nothing in the hang or unilateral-training literature
  shows that acting without a measurement helps. This is the same structural
  finding `pressing-asymmetry.md` reached for the OHP footage, independently.
- **Passive dead hangs have no hypertrophy evidence for the lats and no
  evidence connecting them to correcting an asymmetry, anywhere.** A relaxed
  hang puts the lats under passive stretch but produces minimal active EMG —
  there is no concentric or eccentric muscle action for the prime movers to
  adapt to. I found no trial, in any population, that measures lat girth,
  thickness, or asymmetry before/after a hanging intervention. The
  shoulder-health and spinal-decompression claims commonly made for dead
  hangs are either genuinely mechanistic but unrelated to muscle (disc
  unloading under traction, well established in general) or unverifiable as
  cited above. Grip endurance is the one outcome with real trial support.
- **Active hangs (scapular depression held in a dead hang) are a real
  scapular-control drill family** — mechanistically close to a push-up-plus
  and a scapular pull-up, both of which have genuine EMG support for
  recruiting lower trapezius and serratus anterior. But as a *diagnostic* for
  asymmetry, an active hang inherits the exact problem static photos have:
  visual rating of scapular position is close to unreliable even among
  trained raters (kappa 0.12–0.61 across four studies, one reaching
  -0.20 to 0.16), and belief in the construct predicts the rating more than
  the shoulder does. Scapular asymmetry at rest is also simply common —
  28–42% prevalence in asymptomatic non-overhead populations — so "I can see
  a difference" is close to the base rate, not a finding.
- **The one real framework built for exactly this question — side-to-side
  scapular position — is the Lateral Scapular Slide Test (LSST).** It is a
  genuine, tape-measured, three-position test, not a photo guess. But like
  the Limb Symmetry Index the project already debunked for a different joint,
  its normative claim ("<1.0 cm is symmetric") rests on Kibler's own largely
  unpublished data in overhead throwers, later studies found poor specificity
  (28–58%) and inconsistent inter-rater ICCs (0.43–0.85 depending on arm
  position), and multiple authors now say it should not be used to screen for
  shoulder dysfunction in other populations. It is the right-shaped tool and
  the wrong validated number — exactly the physique-metrics.md LSI pattern,
  recurring.
- **Unilateral training does not "fix" an asymmetry by some different growth
  mechanism — it removes the mechanical path by which one side can be carried
  by the other.** Kassiano et al. 2025 (systematic review and meta-analysis,
  9 studies; already used in `pressing-asymmetry.md`): unilateral and
  bilateral training produce equivalent hypertrophy; strength adapts
  specifically to the pattern trained (bilateral training → better bilateral
  strength, unilateral → better unilateral strength). So the growth stimulus
  per working muscle is the same either way; what changes with an
  independently-loaded unilateral exercise is that the weak side's own force
  output, not the strong side's, now determines the load and the rep count.
- **"Compensatory dominance" in bilateral back exercises is a real,
  biomechanically plausible phenomenon for shared-implement lifts, directly
  measured in the one closely analogous lift that has been studied — but not
  measured for rows or pulldowns themselves.** The bench-press offset-loading
  literature this project already found (`pressing-asymmetry.md`: n=10
  bodybuilders, dominant pec 51% vs 35% MVIC under *symmetric* bilateral
  loading; a 4-week ultrasound RCT in which offset loading closed a
  dominant/non-dominant pec growth gap) is the nearest comparator. I searched
  specifically for an equivalent row/pulldown study and found none — the
  mechanism (one side of a rigid shared bar doing more of the work) is
  plausible by close mechanical analogy to bench press, but this is
  **inference, not evidence**, and the magnitude could easily be smaller or
  larger given rows are pulled, not pressed, and most machine rows (including,
  typically, a chest-supported row) use two independently-loaded handles
  rather than one rigid bar, which removes the mechanism the bench-press
  result depends on.
- **The single trained-lifter EMG study that compares unilateral and
  bilateral rowing directly found no activation difference between them** (n
  = 30, trained men, 80% 1RM bent-over row, lats/rear delt/biceps EMG) — it
  does not test asymmetry correction, but it is evidence against "unilateral
  rowing works the muscle harder," one of the intuitive claims under this
  proposal.
- **Nothing in this literature supports adding sets.** Every lever that
  matters here — equal reps per side, a weak-side-first convention, swapping
  an exercise within its existing slot — is a substitution question, not a
  volume one, and SYNTHESIS §2.1's flat-volume decision was made deliberately
  and enforced against this same athlete earlier the same day.

## Where the proposal is wrong, unsupported, or premature

1. **"Add more unilateral exercises" as a general response to a visual
   impression of asymmetry** has no evidence behind the word "fix." Unilateral
   training's real, evidenced effect is to stop one side's output being set by
   the other's — it is a measurement-honesty tool and a load-sharing fix for a
   *confirmed* deficit, not a corrective exercise with its own growth
   mechanism. Absent a confirmed deficit, converting an exercise to unilateral
   format buys nothing (Kassiano 2025: no hypertrophy difference) and costs
   session time, because every unilateral set takes roughly twice as long per
   working set as its bilateral equivalent at matched volume.
2. **Dead hangs are being asked to do work (correct an asymmetry, build
   lats) that nothing in the literature shows them doing.** The two most
   specific claims found in general search for hang-specific shoulder benefits
   could not be verified and look fabricated. What is real — spinal unloading,
   grip endurance — is unrelated to the question asked.
3. **"The active hang will show me the asymmetry" treats a dynamic, gravity-
   loaded version of the same visual-asymmetry question `physique-metrics.md`
   already answered for static photos.** The underlying problem — human visual
   rating of scapular/shoulder position is unreliable, and belief shapes the
   rating — does not go away because the body is hanging rather than posed. It
   is a slightly better **test**, for the reasons in Q1 below, but it is not a
   measurement, and should not be reported as one.
4. **Converting chest-supported row to one-arm-DB-row as the *default*** would
   either double its session time (3×8–12 per side) or halve its per-side dose
   if kept at 3 total alternating sets — the first breaks the 60–75-minute
   session constraint and the flat-volume rule by stealth (more total reps and
   fatigue cost per muscle even if "sets" are miscounted), the second is a
   worse stimulus than what exists today. Neither is defensible without a
   confirmed deficit to justify the cost. This mirrors the OHP conclusion in
   `pressing-asymmetry.md` almost exactly, and for the same reason: the
   swap-trigger is a replicated test result, not a feeling.

## Findings

### Q1 — Dead hangs and active hangs

#### 1a. Passive / dead hang

**What it actually loads.** A relaxed dead hang places the shoulder in full
flexion/abduction under body-weight traction, lengthening the lat, teres
major, and pec minor passively. There is no active concentric or eccentric
phase for the lats to adapt to — the muscle is not shortening or
controlling a lengthening under load, it is simply being stretched while the
grip, forearm flexors, and to a lesser extent the rotator cuff and scapular
stabilisers hold the position isometrically.

- **Hypertrophy: no evidence found, any muscle, any population.** I searched
  specifically for a dead-hang hypertrophy trial and found none. The passive-
  stretch hypertrophy literature that exists (lengthened partials, long-
  muscle-length training — already covered for this athlete's priority
  muscles in `research/hypertrophy.md` §3 and `SYNTHESIS.md` §2.3) is built on
  *actively loaded* lengthened positions (lengthened-partial curls, Romanian
  deadlifts, lengthened leg extensions) — tension at long muscle length, not
  tension-free passive stretch. A dead hang is the latter. Treat the stretch-
  mediated-hypertrophy literature as **not transferable** to a dead hang;
  this is a mechanistic absence, not a weak positive. **Confidence: high that
  no direct evidence exists; high that the mechanistic case for meaningful lat
  growth from a dead hang specifically is weak** — EIMD/mechanical-tension
  models of hypertrophy require active tension, which a relaxed hang does not
  provide.
- **Grip endurance: real, trial-supported, irrelevant to the question asked.**
  Rock-climbing hangboard literature (e.g., a 4-week RCT, n = 54 experienced
  climbers, comparing 100%/80%/60%-intensity weighted-hang protocols) finds
  endurance and strength adaptations from *weighted, near-maximal* hangs, not
  idle dead hangs — and the outcome measured is finger/forearm grip, not
  shoulder or lat tissue. **Tier 2-adjacent (RCT), climbers not lifters,
  irrelevant muscle group for this question.**
- **Shoulder health / "decompression": thin, and partly unverifiable as
  cited.** General disc-traction mechanics (reduced intradiscal pressure under
  axial unloading) are real and old findings (Nachemson-era and later work),
  but the specific hang-and-shoulder-ROM number that circulates in blog
  content could not be traced to a real paper (see Access note). What legitimate
  evidence exists for "hanging helps the shoulder" is association and
  expert opinion (climbing and calisthenics coaching, tier 5), not a measured
  trial outcome.
- **Asymmetry: zero literature found, in either direction.** No study
  anywhere connects dead hangs — passive or otherwise — to detecting,
  preventing, or correcting a size or strength asymmetry. This is the honest
  absence, not a hedge.
- **Verdict: the appeal is real but is grip/shoulder-mobility/CARs-adjacent
  folklore for the hypertrophy and asymmetry questions specifically, and
  thinly verified even for the shoulder-health claim.** Your prior was right.

#### 1b. Active hang

**What it actually is.** A dead hang with the shoulder blades actively
depressed/retracted (pulled down and slightly back) while the arms stay
straight — mechanically identical to a scapular pull-up's bottom-to-mid
phase held isometrically, and closely related to a push-up-plus in loading
pattern (just inverted and traction- rather than compression-loaded).

- **It is a genuine scapular-control drill family, with real (if indirect)
  EMG support.** Push-up-plus variants reliably recruit upper and lower
  serratus anterior and lower trapezius more than a static elevation hold
  (multiple EMG studies, trained and mixed populations, tier 4). Scapular
  pull-ups and active hangs are the inverted, traction-loaded analogue and
  are widely taught as a pull-up progression on that basis — I found no EMG
  study of the active hang itself, only of its close relatives, so this is
  **inference by mechanical analogy, moderate confidence**, not a direct
  measurement.
- **As a hypertrophy input: trivial and not the point.** The load is body
  weight through a tiny range, well below what the program's other lat/rear-
  delt work already provides. It is a control/activation drill, not a growth
  stimulus, and should never be counted toward any §2.1 fractional band.
- **As a diagnostic: the same confound as a static photo, with one partial
  mitigation.** The confound is the one `physique-metrics.md` already
  established: what the eye reads as "one shoulder blade sits lower/more
  winged" could be true asymmetry, could be habitual posture, and visual
  rating of scapular position is close to unreliable even among trained
  raters — four independent studies report kappa from 0.12 to 0.61, one
  reaching **−0.20 to 0.16** (worse than chance), and the newest of them
  (Vila-Dieguez 2026, 104 physiotherapists rating 5 videos of healthy
  shoulders) found raters who believed scapular dyskinesis mattered were more
  likely to see it — belief, not shoulder, driving the rating. Scapular
  asymmetry at rest is also just common: 28–42% prevalence of visible
  dyskinesis in asymptomatic, non-overhead-sport populations (Burn 2016, n =
  1,401 across 12 studies, already used in `pressing-asymmetry.md`), so
  seeing *something* is closer to the base rate than a finding.
  **The partial mitigation:** a relaxed/active hang removes the voluntary
  postural correction a person makes when consciously posing for a camera
  (`physique-metrics.md`'s stated confound for static shots), so what you see
  in a hang is closer to an unguarded resting position than a posed
  double-biceps shot is. That is a genuine, if small, advantage over a
  photograph — it does not fix the rating-reliability problem, but it removes
  one layer of confound the photo has and the hang does not.
- **The one real validated-ish framework for exactly this question — and its
  limits.** Kibler's Lateral Scapular Slide Test (LSST) measures the distance
  from the inferior angle of each scapula to a fixed spinous process at three
  arm positions, including one essentially identical to a relaxed hang (arms
  at the side). It is a genuine tape-measured protocol, not a visual guess,
  with published intra-rater reliability (test-retest ratio 0.84–0.88) and
  inter-rater reliability (0.77–0.85) in Kibler's own reporting. But: the
  "<1.0 cm = normal" cutoff traces to largely unpublished data in overhead
  *throwing* athletes, not a general or lifting population; independent
  studies since have found poor specificity (28–58% depending on arm
  position and criterion) and inconsistent interrater ICCs (0.43–0.85 across
  positions and shoulder-health status); and multiple sources now explicitly
  say the LSST should not be used to screen for shoulder dysfunction outside
  the population it was built on. **This is the exact pattern
  `physique-metrics.md` already found for the Limb Symmetry Index** — a real
  test, an unvalidated-for-this-population threshold — and it recurs here
  because both fields reach for a round-number cutoff before the number has
  been tested outside the original cohort.
- **Can it serve as a crude, free, at-home diagnostic — yes, with the
  measurement-noise discipline the project already uses elsewhere, not with
  the literature's threshold.** Concretely: an active hang filmed from behind,
  repeated on two separate occasions at least a few days apart, is a free
  <2-minute check. Apply the same rule `physique-metrics.md` and
  `pressing-asymmetry.md` both already use for exactly this reason — a
  difference only counts if it (a) replicates in the same direction on two
  separate days, and (b) is larger than the take-to-take variation you see
  re-filming the same hang twice in a row. That is not the LSST's 1.0 cm
  convention; it is the athlete's own noise floor, which is the more
  defensible number in both of the prior files for the same reason it is
  here.
- **Where it fits without violating the flat-volume rule: a periodic test,
  not a programmed exercise.** An active hang used this way costs under two
  minutes, no sets, no reps, no slot — it is the same category as the OHP
  re-film protocol in `pressing-asymmetry.md` (re-assess once per 4-week
  block). It should never appear in the program as a prescribed exercise
  with sets/reps; if it did, every set would need the same accounting the
  other exercises get, for a drill with no established hypertrophy or
  corrective value. If the athlete wants to keep doing active-hang holds
  anyway (as a warm-up, a grip thing, or because he likes the drill), that is
  a free, zero-count addition exactly like the cost-free micro-adjustment
  `pressing-asymmetry.md` recommended for the OHP — not a decision for this
  file to block, just not a programmed training stimulus either.

### Q2 — Unilateral vs bilateral exercise selection for a perceived asymmetry

#### 2a. Does unilateral training "fix" a pre-existing asymmetry, mechanistically?

**Finding: it does not grow a weak side faster through some separate
mechanism — it removes one specific mechanical path by which the strong side
can carry the weak one, and it is only that path, not an inherent one, that
determines whether anything changes.**

- **Hypertrophy is equivalent between unilateral and bilateral training at
  matched volume and effort.** Kassiano et al. 2025, *Sports Medicine*
  (systematic review + meta-analysis, 9 studies; cited directly in
  `pressing-asymmetry.md`, re-used here, not re-verified independently this
  pass): no significant difference in muscle growth between unilateral and
  bilateral training; strength gains are specific to the pattern trained
  (bilateral training produces larger bilateral-strength gains, unilateral
  produces larger unilateral-strength gains). **Confidence: moderate**, read
  via abstract-level detail in the prior file, population mixed across
  included trials. **This directly answers "does loading each side
  independently correct a pre-existing difference by letting the weak side
  work at its own true capacity" — only if a load-sharing problem exists on
  the bilateral version in the first place.** If the bilateral exercise
  already delivers equal load to both sides (e.g., two independent dumbbells,
  two independent cable handles), there is no carried-side mechanism for
  unilateral training to remove, and Kassiano's null result is the whole
  story: no difference, no reason to switch.
- **The EMG evidence specific to rowing finds no activation difference
  between unilateral and bilateral performance of the same row pattern.**
  Lee et al., *AIP Conference Proceedings* 2023 (n = 30 recreationally
  active, resistance-trained men, 20–23 y, 80% 1RM bent-over row, randomised
  order): no significant EMG difference in lats, posterior deltoid or biceps
  between unilateral and bilateral bent-over rows. **Confidence: low-moderate**
  — single small study, abstract-level detail only, young and only
  "recreationally active," and it tests per-rep activation of the *working*
  muscle, not left-right load-sharing (it did not measure left vs right
  separately). It is useful mainly to knock down the specific claim "doing it
  one arm at a time works the muscle harder" — it does not.
- **A separate, real phenomenon — the bilateral deficit — is not the same
  thing as a side-to-side asymmetry, and conflating them is a mistake worth
  naming explicitly.** The bilateral deficit is the finding that maximal
  force output summed across both limbs contracting *together* is often less
  than the sum of each limb's maximal output measured *separately*,
  attributed mainly to interhemispheric inhibition (narrative/systematic
  reviews, e.g. PMC9413577). It describes total output being left on the
  table when both sides work at once — it says nothing about which side is
  stronger, and training emphasis shifts it in both directions (more
  bilateral training reduces the deficit, more unilateral training increases
  it, i.e. unilateral and bilateral performance diverge further with
  unilateral-biased training). **This is tier 1–2 adjacent, well established
  as a phenomenon, but it is not evidence for or against correcting an
  asymmetry — it is a different question the athlete's proposal risks
  importing by name alone.**

#### 2b. "Compensatory dominance" in bilateral back exercises specifically

**Finding: a real, biomechanically coherent phenomenon for a shared, rigid
implement — directly measured in the single closest analogous lift (bench
press) — but not measured for rows or pulldowns, and the mechanism does not
obviously apply to equipment that already loads each hand independently.**

- **What is actually measured, and where.** The project already found (and
  I re-use rather than re-verify) that under *symmetric* bilateral bench-press
  loading, the dominant pec and anterior delt carry substantially more of the
  work by EMG (51% vs 35% MVIC, n = 10 trained bodybuilders, Frontiers 2025),
  that ordinary bilateral bench training over 4 weeks grows the dominant pec
  more (0.20 vs 0.13 cm, n = 21 men with 5+ years' experience), and that a
  4-week offset-loading intervention (loading the weak side ~2–5% more)
  closed that growth gap in one RCT. This is the one piece of this project's
  evidence base that directly shows a bilateral lift can let a strong side do
  disproportionate work, and that a targeted fix changes the outcome.
- **I searched specifically for a row or pulldown equivalent and found
  none.** No offset-loading, no asymmetric-EMG, no left-right-comparison study
  on a row or pulldown exists in the literature I could find. The claim that
  "rows and pulldowns let the strong side compensate" circulates widely as
  gym-culture convention (general fitness-content search results, tier 6,
  no citation behind them) but has never been tested the way bench press has.
- **Whether the bench-press mechanism transfers is a real, open question, not
  a safe extrapolation, and the answer plausibly depends on the specific
  equipment.** A barbell bench press is one rigid bar gripped by both hands —
  if one side pushes harder, the bar can still move in a controlled path
  while the weak side does less work and nobody notices. A chest-supported
  row or lat pulldown on most commercial machines uses **two independently
  loaded handles or cables**, not one rigid bar; if that is the equipment
  this athlete actually uses, the mechanical precondition for the bench-press
  finding (one rigid shared object, load distributed by whoever pulls harder)
  is largely absent, because each hand's resistance is fixed regardless of
  how hard the other side pulls. A barbell bent-over row, by contrast, *is* a
  single rigid bar and the mechanism could apply there exactly as it does to
  bench press. **This matters for the program and is not resolved here** —
  which specific row/pulldown apparatus the athlete uses determines whether
  the pressing-asymmetry precedent transfers at all. I flag it rather than
  guess, because guessing wrongly in either direction (assuming it transfers
  when the equipment already isolates sides, or assuming it doesn't when the
  equipment is a shared bar) leads to the wrong recommendation below.
- **Verdict: plausible for shared-bar movements, unverified for machine rows
  and pulldowns, and not established as a real finding for this athlete's
  specific equipment without knowing which it is.** This is squarely
  "pressing-specific, not confirmed transferable," the possibility the
  question asked me to be honest about.

#### 2c. What actually fixes a confirmed deficit, versus what is assumed

**Finding: if a real unilateral strength deficit is ever confirmed (not
assumed), the evidence supports training both unilateral and bilateral,
with extra volume or extra effort on the weak side specifically — not simply
switching to unilateral-only or matching the strong side down.**

- **Extra-volume-on-the-weak-side protocols are the only ones with controlled
  outcome data**, and they are lower-limb and athletic: a 10-week RCT (n = 30
  university basketball players) giving the weaker leg 3 sets to the
  dominant leg's 1 (unilateral squat-pattern work, 3×/week) cut jump-height
  asymmetry from 15.25% to 7.86% and isometric mid-thigh-pull asymmetry from
  9.07% to 3.20%, against no change in controls (Frontiers in Physiology
  2024, already used in `pressing-asymmetry.md`). A 6-week female-handball RCT
  reports a similar direction (quad asymmetry 13.19%→7.60%, hamstring
  16.65%→7.83%). **No upper-body or pulling equivalent exists.** The dose (3:1
  sets) is also directly incompatible with this programme's flat-volume
  decision, so even if an upper-body analogue existed, importing the dose
  would need to come from the reviewer, not be assumed.
- **"Train the weak side first, match the strong side to it" is, as
  `pressing-asymmetry.md` already concluded for pressing, a coaching
  convention with no controlled test — cheap, plausible, unproven.** The same
  verdict applies to pulling; nothing about rows makes the ordering question
  different from pressing.
- **Matching the strong side down to the weak side's reps (rather than giving
  the weak side extra volume) has no outcome evidence either way** that I
  could find, for any limb or any exercise family.
- **Time course, if it ever applies: a 4-week block for a first check, 8–10
  weeks for a real verdict** — same horizon `pressing-asymmetry.md`
  recommended for the OHP, because the underlying RCTs run on that timescale
  and nothing specific to pulling changes it.

#### 2d. For this athlete's actual pull day

**Finding: converting chest-supported row to one-arm-DB-row as the default,
or adding a unilateral lat-isolation movement, is solving a problem that has
not been shown to exist, and both carry a real cost against this specific
programme's stated constraints.**

- **No confirmed deficit exists.** The entire chain this proposal rests on —
  real asymmetry → it is a strength/growth asymmetry → unilateral work
  corrects it — is unestablished at every link, exactly as
  `pressing-asymmetry.md` found for the shoulder's cousin question. A felt
  visual impression plus an unmeasured sense that "the lats look uneven" is
  not a replicated finding, by this project's own stated and applied
  standard from earlier in the same day (the tape "under band" reading was
  ruled an adherence artifact rather than a finding, on weaker inferential
  grounds than this).
- **The honest cost of the swap.** Chest-supported row runs 3×8–12 at
  roughly 90–120 s rest. Converting to one-arm-DB-row at the same sets-and-
  reps-per-side roughly doubles the exercise's time slot (each side is its own
  working set); done at 3 *total* alternating sets instead, the per-side dose
  halves relative to today. Either breaks something the programme already
  protects — session time (BRIEF: 60–75 minutes) or the stimulus a set is
  meant to deliver — for a hypothesis with no supporting measurement.
- **Whether it would even hit the mechanism this file found plausible depends
  on equipment this file cannot see.** If the athlete's chest-supported row
  already uses two independently loaded handles, converting to dumbbells
  changes almost nothing mechanically that bears on compensatory dominance —
  both are already per-side-independent. The one case where a swap would
  plausibly do something the current exercise doesn't is if the row is
  performed on a single rigid handle/bar machine, which is the bench-press-
  analogous condition. This needs checking before any decision, not assuming.
- **`one-arm-db-row` already existing as a swap alternative is the right
  design, and should stay exactly where it is** — available, not default.
  This matches `pressing-asymmetry.md`'s structure for the OHP precisely:
  keep the bilateral lift as the default and the unilateral variant as the
  trigger-gated substitution, not the other way round.
- **Adding a unilateral lat-isolation movement** fails the same test
  `pull-day-vertical-redundancy.md` already applied to the lat-pulldown
  slot: no tier-1/2 evidence distinguishes unilateral from bilateral lat
  isolation on hypertrophy outcome (Kassiano 2025, above), it would add sets
  against SYNTHESIS §2.1's deliberate flat-volume decision, and back is
  explicitly a maintenance muscle in this programme (BRIEF: "everything else
  is maintained"). There is no gap to fill.

## Recommendations for this athlete

1. **No program change now.** Neither dead hangs, active hangs, nor a
   unilateral-row default clears the bar this project has applied all day:
   a replicated measurement, not a feeling.
2. **One free, <2-minute test, repeatable without equipment or logging:** an
   active hang (shoulders actively depressed, arms straight), filmed from
   behind, on two separate days at least a few days apart. Compare scapular
   position side-to-side against the spine. **Count it as a finding only if
   the same-direction difference replicates on both days and exceeds the
   take-to-take spread from re-filming the same hang twice in a row** — the
   same noise-floor discipline `physique-metrics.md` uses for tape and
   `pressing-asymmetry.md` uses for the OHP clips. This is diagnostic, not
   corrective: it costs no sets, no slot, and should never enter the program
   as a prescribed exercise.
3. **If (2) replicates, the next step is a strength test, not an exercise
   swap:** a two-day single-arm test on an existing pulling pattern (single-
   arm cable row or single-arm lat pulldown, same reps-not-load logic as
   `pressing-asymmetry.md` §2b — pick a load giving ~8 reps to near failure,
   test both sides, repeat ≥3 days later, positive only if the same side loses
   by ≥2 reps on both occasions). This directly tests the thing that would
   actually matter (a real strength deficit) rather than inferring one from a
   look.
4. **Before any equipment-based decision, check what the chest-supported row
   actually is** — single shared handle/bar, or two independent handles. This
   determines whether the bench-press compensatory-dominance precedent has
   any mechanical basis to transfer, and nothing in this file can answer it
   without that fact.
5. **If a deficit replicates on (3):** the evidence-supported response is
   equal sets and equal reps per side on an already-available unilateral
   substitute (`one-arm-db-row`, which already exists as a swap — use it, do
   not add to it), logging the weaker side's reps as the limiting number
   exactly as `pressing-asymmetry.md` recommends for the OHP. Do **not**
   adopt the 3:1 weak:strong-side dosing from the lower-limb RCTs without
   reviewer sign-off — it conflicts with the flat-volume decision and has no
   upper-body precedent. Re-test at 4 and 8–10 weeks.
6. **Dead hangs: optional, zero-count, not a program line item.** If the
   athlete wants them for grip or personal preference, they cost nothing to
   add outside the counted sets. They should not be framed, logged, or relied
   upon as addressing the asymmetry question — no evidence connects them to
   it.
7. **If nothing replicates on (2) or (3): close the question**, the same way
   `pressing-asymmetry.md` closes the OHP question absent a positive result,
   and re-check once a block if it still bothers the athlete.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions

- **Whether the chest-supported row and lat pulldown this athlete actually
  uses are single-shared-implement or independently-loaded-per-side** is
  unknown here and changes whether the compensatory-dominance mechanism (2b)
  has any basis to apply at all. This should be checked before recommendation
  5 above is acted on.
- **The two most specific dead-hang citations found by general web search
  (a 2016 Spine Journal disc-pressure figure, a 2019 JPTS shoulder-ROM trial)
  could not be verified and are flagged as likely fabricated** — a caution
  about the broader claim ecosystem around dead hangs online, not just these
  two numbers.
- **No row/pulldown offset-loading or EMG-asymmetry study exists**, so the
  transfer of the bench-press finding (2b) to pulling is inference, not
  evidence, in either direction. If such a study appeared, it would settle
  the question this file could only reason about.
- **The active-hang-as-diagnostic recommendation rests on the athlete's own
  replicated observation, not a validated instrument** — if he reports a
  consistent, large, two-day-replicated difference, that is still only as
  strong as a home test with no published reliability data of its own; it
  should raise, not settle, the question, which is exactly why step 3 (a
  strength test) is the actual gate, not step 2.
- **This file would change if:** a confirmed strength deficit emerged from
  the two-day test (→ recommendation 5 activates); the athlete's row/pulldown
  equipment turned out to be single-handle (→ the compensatory-dominance
  inference gets materially stronger); or pain or any Section-7-type red flag
  from `pressing-asymmetry.md` appeared (→ refer, this file does not apply).
