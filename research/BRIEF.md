## The athlete

**This is the single most important section. Most training research is on
untrained or moderately trained subjects, and its conclusions often do not
transfer to this person. Weight evidence accordingly, and say so explicitly when
a finding rests on a population unlike him.**

- Male, **10+ years of continuous resistance training**, drug-free.
- **Close to his natural limit** for strength and muscle. Assume the easy gains
  are long gone: progress is slow, sensitivity to volume and recovery is high,
  and the cost of a wasted mesocycle is months, not weeks.
- **A novice runner, no longer a complete beginner.** He started at a 10–15
  minute jog and has been following the current plan for some weeks. **Current
  status: about 5 km continuous, roughly 35 minutes at ~9 km/h (~6:40/km).**
  Heavier and more muscular than a typical novice runner. Treat him as a
  novice with a few weeks of consistent base — the tissues are adapting but are
  not yet a runner's. The remaining journey is 5 km → 10 km, not 0 → 10 km.
- **Core is essentially untrained** relative to the rest of him. Strong lifter,
  but has never trained the trunk directly. **Critical adherence constraint:**
  he found the mat-based work in the current program — dead bug, bird dog,
  planks — boring enough that he started skipping it. He prefers exercises
  that use cables, machines or a bench. A core progression he will not do is
  worth nothing, whatever the evidence says about it; adherence is the binding
  constraint here, and the research must respect it.
- Trains 4 fixed days a week, will do a 5th only as an optional bonus.
- Exact current working loads are not supplied. Treat him as advanced and give
  guidance that holds across plausible advanced-level loads.

## The goals, in priority order

1. **Add load to three lifts:** incline barbell bench press, standing barbell
   overhead press, weighted pull-up. These are the strength goals. They are not
   powerlifting lifts and he is not competing.
2. **Hypertrophy of shoulders and arms** — side delts, rear delts, biceps,
   triceps — as the priority muscle groups. Everything else is maintained.
3. **Run a continuous 10K**, from the current ~5 km / 35 minute base.
4. **Build a trained core** from scratch, safely, alongside all of the above —
   using exercises he will actually do (cables, machines, bench), not mat work.

Legs are trained for maintenance and health, not maximised: running is also leg
work, and leg fatigue competes with the long run.

## Hard constraints

- 4 lifting sessions per week, on fixed days. A 5th is optional and must never be
  required for the program to work.
- 2 runs per week, optionally 3.
- Core work rides along on non-lifting days or at the end of sessions.
- No equipment constraints beyond a normally equipped commercial gym.
- Sessions should fit in roughly 60–75 minutes.

### Core: 3 phases, advanced by sessions completed

1. **Motor control** (sessions 0–11): dead bug 3×, bird dog 3×, side plank 3×25s,
   front plank 3×35s. McGill "Big 3".
2. **Capacity** (12–26): front plank 3×60s, side plank 3×45s, Pallof press 3×,
   hanging knee raise 3×, dead bug 2×.
3. **Loaded** (27+): ab wheel 3×, hanging leg raise 3×, cable crunch 3×, Pallof

**This progression failed on adherence.** Phase 1 is entirely mat work and he
stopped doing it. The design assumption — earn loaded work by first building
bracing endurance on the floor — may be sound physiologically and still be the
wrong prescription for this person. The research should establish whether a
strong lifter with an untrained trunk actually needs the mat phase, and what a
cable / machine / bench-based progression looks like from day one.

### Research the current program cites

Grgic 2018 (frequency, volume-equated); Colquhoun 2018 (3× vs 6× bench); Rhea

Check these. Some may be misread, out of date, or over-applied.

## Evidence standards

Rank sources in this order, and say which tier each finding comes from:

1. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews, especially those that subgroup by
   training status.
2. RCTs in **trained** subjects (2+ years, ideally more). For this athlete,
   one good study in trained lifters outweighs several in novices.
3. RCTs in untrained subjects — usable, but flag the transfer problem explicitly.
4. Acute / mechanistic studies (MPS, EMG, muscle damage markers). Suggestive, not
   conclusive; do not let these drive recommendations on their own.
5. Expert practitioners with a track record and stated reasoning (e.g. Helms,
   Israetel, Nuckols, Schoenfeld, Beardsley for lifting; Daniels, Pfitzinger,
   Magness for running). Cite as expert opinion, not evidence.
6. General blogs and content marketing. Avoid unless they cite (1)–(3), in which
   case cite the underlying source instead.

For **every** finding, record:

- Source with a link
- Study design and sample size
- **Population training status** — untrained / trained / advanced / competitive
- Direction and, where available, effect size
- Your confidence: **high / moderate / low**, and why

Distinguish clearly between *"the evidence shows"*, *"expert consensus holds"*,
and *"I am inferring"*. Where studies conflict, present the conflict; do not
average it away. Prefer recent work (2022–2026) where the field has moved — for
example on lengthened-position training, proximity to failure, and running
injury load models — but do not discard older foundational work.

Search broad first, then narrow. Expect to run 15–30 searches and read the
primary source for anything you rely on heavily. If a claim cannot be traced to
a source you can actually open, say so rather than citing it from memory.

## Output

Write your findings to the file named in your instructions, under `research/`.
Use this structure:

```markdown
# <Domain> — Research Findings

## Summary
Up to ten bullets. What matters most for THIS athlete.

## Where the current program is wrong, unsupported, or suboptimal
Be specific. Quote the program element, say what the evidence indicates instead,
and how confident you are. This is the section the synthesis will lean on.

## Findings
One subsection per question in your instructions. Under each: the finding,
then the evidence lines (source · design · population · n · effect · confidence).

## Recommendations for this athlete
Concrete and actionable. Sets, reps, frequencies, sequencing, numbers. Written
so the program can be changed from them directly.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions
Where the evidence disagrees or runs out. Where your recommendation would change
if a fact about the athlete turned out differently.

## Sources
Full list, with links.
```

When you finish, return to the lead agent **no more than eight lines**: the path
of the file you wrote, the three to five findings that matter most, and the
single biggest way the current program should change. Do not paste the file.

## Boundaries

Stay inside your domain. Other specialists are covering the rest, and overlap
wastes effort while gaps lose findings. Your own instructions say what is yours
and what is not. If something outside your scope is load-bearing for a
conclusion inside it, note it in a sentence and move on.

Do not modify anything under `src/`, `tests/`, or `tools/`. Do not change the
program. You are researching, not implementing.
