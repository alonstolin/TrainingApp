# Strength Progression — Research Findings

Scope: progression on incline barbell bench press, standing barbell overhead press and weighted pull-up for an advanced (10+ yr) drug-free lifter, plus the trunk progression for a strong lifter with an untrained core who will not do mat work. Accessory hypertrophy volume, running, and lifting/running scheduling belong to other specialists; where scheduling is load-bearing for a strength conclusion it is flagged in one line.

Evidence tiers follow the brief: T1 meta-analysis/systematic review; T2 RCT in trained; T3 RCT in untrained; T4 acute/mechanistic; T5 expert practice/consensus; T6 blogs (avoided).

## Summary

- **The single biggest correction: the back-off sets are where the strength stimulus lives, and the program's back-off prescription (3×6 @ 85% of the day's top set) is both too light and anchored to the noisiest number in the session.** In competitive powerlifters a daily heavy single alone produced no meaningful gain over 6 weeks (6% probability), while the same single plus two back-off triples at 80% produced a clear gain (99.6%) (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021, T2). A 4–6 rep top set at RPE 7.5 is ≈ 78–80% 1RM; 85% of that is ≈ 67% 1RM — sets of 6 at RPE ~4–5 for a trained lifter. The back-offs need to sit at ~75–85% 1RM / RPE 7–8, prescribed from a stable block reference, not from whatever the top set did that day.
- **Chasing a daily max is worse than submaximal relative-intensity work in well-trained lifters** (Carroll 2019, n=15 well-trained, 10 wk: relative-intensity group > repetition-maximum group for strength, RFD and jump, with lower training strain — T2). The top set should be a *probe and record*, capped at RPE 8–8.5 in most weeks.
- **RPE/RIR autoregulation is one of the program's stronger choices.** Trained lifters select loads to within ~0.3 RPE of target (Helms 2017), and RPE-based loading matched or slightly beat percentage-based loading in every trained-lifter trial (Helms 2018 ES 0.28–0.50; Graham & Cleather 2021 significant; Hickmott 2022 pooled SMD 0.30, p=0.06). Moderate confidence.
- **DUP is defensible, not uniquely supported.** In trained subgroups undulating beats linear (Moesgaard 2022, ES 0.61, CI touches 0); block was equal-or-better and more efficient per unit volume in athletes (Painter 2012; Bartolomei 2014). What matters is that intensity varies within the week and the heaviest session comes when freshest (Zourdos 2016).
- **Two exposures per lift per week is right; "one heavy + one volume" is a convention.** Volume-equated meta-analyses in trained lifters find no independent frequency effect (Grgic 2018 p=0.42; Cuthbert 2021 g≈0.06–0.09); the newest meta-regression finds a modest positive frequency effect with diminishing returns (Pelland 2025). Three exposures would add little unless weekly volume also rises.
- **Strength gain is insensitive to how close to failure sets are taken, across a wide RIR range (Robinson 2024, 67 studies); hypertrophy is not.** The RPE 9 week buys no extra strength stimulus over RPE 8; it is a test week, and should be treated as such. Heavy loads (>60% 1RM, in practice 75–90%) do matter for 1RM (Schoenfeld 2017).
- **Per-lift weekly dose: strength shows steep diminishing returns above ~4–5 direct sets/week in general populations (Pelland 2025), but in competitive powerlifters 1–3 direct sets/week was insufficient and 3–9 sufficient (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021).** The program's ~7 sets per lift per week is in the right window for an advanced lifter.
- **Realistic rate: the strongest quartile of male powerlifters gained ~0.10 kg/day on the three-lift total over 15 years of competition (~37 kg/yr on the total, ≈ 12 kg/yr per lift; less for upper-body lifts) (Latella 2020, n=1897).** For this athlete, one 1.25–2.5 kg increment per lift per 5-week block is a good outcome; micro-loading (1.25 kg) is warranted on OHP and pull-ups, optional on incline.
- **Deloads: a fixed every-5th-week reduced-load week matches what competitive strength athletes do (every 5.6 ± 2.3 wk, 6.4 days, fewer sets/reps, lower load and effort, same frequency and exercises — Rogerson 2024, n=246) and the Bell 2023 expert Delphi. The only trained-lifter RCT tested a full rest week and found it cost lower-body strength (Coleman 2024).** Keep the reduced-load design; make the timing reactive with a calendar ceiling.
- **Core: no evidence requires a floor-based bracing-endurance phase before loaded trunk work in a healthy, strong lifter.** The best injury-prevention evidence in *novice runners* (Leppänen 2024 BJSM, n=325, LE injury HR 0.66) comes from an 8-exercise hip-and-core programme in which 6 of 8 exercises are hip/leg strength (lunges, band deadlifts, Nordics, miniband abduction, bridges) that this athlete already trains harder than the trial did; only 2 are trunk holds. A cable/machine/bench progression from day one loses very little — mainly the passive-stiffness effect of long isometric holds (Lee & McGill 2015, T4), which loaded isometrics (Pallof press, suitcase carry, standing OHP) substantially replace.

## Where the current program is wrong, unsupported, or suboptimal

1. **"Top set 4–6 @ RPE target, then 3×6 @ 85% of that top set."** Three problems. (a) *Too light.* A 6-rep set at RPE 7.5 is ~2.5 RIR ≈ an 8–9 RM ≈ 78–80% 1RM; 85% of that ≈ 66–68% 1RM for sets of 6 ≈ RPE 4–5. Even in week 4 (4 reps @ RPE 9 ≈ 87% 1RM) the back-offs land at ≈ 74% 1RM ≈ RPE 7. For a trained lifter, sets at RPE 4–5 are sub-threshold for strength (Androulakis-Korakakis 2020: the effective minimum in trained men is ~70–85% 1RM at high effort). (b) *Wrong anchor.* RPE accuracy in trained lifters is good near failure and worse further from it (Helms 2017: bench press RPE more accurate at 3 reps @ 9 than 2 reps @ 8), so an RPE 7.5 top set is the least reliable number in the session, and everything downstream is derived from it. A bad day cuts the dose exactly when a stable stimulus matters most; a good day inflates it. (c) *Inverted logic.* The trained-lifter evidence (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021; Carroll 2019) says the top set is the probe and the sub-maximal sets are the stimulus; the program treats it the other way round. **Confidence: high** that the back-offs are too light in weeks 1–3; **high** that the back-off volume is the main stimulus; **moderate** that anchoring to the daily top set harms progression.
2. **The RPE ramp 7.5 → 8 → 8.5 → 9.** Strength gain is insensitive to RIR (Robinson 2024, T1) and a daily-max approach in well-trained lifters produced worse outcomes than submaximal relative-intensity work (Carroll 2019, T2). The week-4 RPE 9 top set is a legitimate *test*; it is not extra stimulus and should not feed the next block's loads as if it were an ordinary session. Also, because the RPE ceiling rises weekly, "clear the range at or under the week's RPE" gets easier each week, so load increases cluster in week 4 on the most fatigue-inflated set, and week 1 of the next block starts heavier at a lower RPE ceiling → a built-in miss. **Confidence: moderate.**
3. **"Heavy day + volume day" is presented as an evidence-based structure.** It is an HL-style DUP convention. What the evidence supports is (i) varying intensity within the week in trained lifters (Moesgaard 2022) and (ii) doing the heaviest session when freshest (Zourdos 2016, HPS > HSP for bench 1RM in powerlifters). Heavy incline on Wednesday after a Tuesday run and heavy OHP on Sunday after the Saturday long run both put the heaviest press the day after a run; the concurrent-training specialist owns this, but it is load-bearing for strength progression. **Confidence: moderate.**
4. **Volume day at 3×8–12 (incline) / 3×8–10 (OHP).** Heavy loads (>60% 1RM) beat light loads for 1RM even when hypertrophy is equal (Schoenfeld 2017, T1), and the program already assigns arm/delt hypertrophy to accessories. For an advanced lifter whose goal on these lifts is *load*, 3–4×6–8 at RPE 7–8 (≈ 72–80% 1RM) is more specific than 8–12. **Confidence: moderate** (specificity principle plus T1 load meta; no direct trial in this population).
5. **Weighted pull-up: "85% of top set" and e1RM logic.** The pull-up load–velocity and load–%1RM relationships hold when load is expressed as *system mass* (bodyweight + external) (Muñoz-López 2017, n=82 trained; Sánchez-Moreno 2017). If percentages or e1RM are computed on the added weight alone they are wrong by a large factor. **Confidence: high.**
6. **Fixed every-5th-week deload regardless of state.** Consistent with practice (Rogerson 2024) and consensus (Bell 2023), but that is T5 evidence; the one trained-lifter RCT of a mid-block week off found a strength cost (Coleman 2024). At ~7 direct sets per lift per week, accumulated fatigue is modest; a reactive trigger with a calendar ceiling is at least as defensible. **Confidence: low–moderate** (evidence thin either way).
7. **Progression increments.** 2.5 kg on a ~70–90 kg standing OHP is a ~3% jump, which at an advanced rate of ~1 kg/month per lift means each increment represents ~2–3 months of adaptation; expect misses. 1.25 kg on OHP is warranted. **Confidence: moderate** (inference from Latella 2020 rates; no micro-loading trial exists).
8. **Citations.** Rhea 2002 is 20 recreationally trained men (T3 for this athlete); the trained-subgroup evidence is Moesgaard 2022. Grgic 2018's frequency effect disappears when volume is equated (p=0.42) and its authors flag that most studies were untrained — it does not support "two exposures beat one" on its own; Pelland 2025 does, modestly. Zourdos 2016 (RIR scale) is a validation study, not outcome evidence; the outcome evidence for RPE loading is Helms 2018 and Graham & Cleather 2021.
9. **Core phase 1 as a mat-based gate.** No evidence located that a floor bracing-endurance phase must precede loaded trunk work in a healthy, strong lifter. The gating logic is borrowed from LBP rehabilitation (McGill's clinical population). The one strong RCT in novice runners (Leppänen 2024) used a hip-and-core programme whose content is mostly loaded hip/leg strength, with progressive resistance "instructed to feel heavy," not low-load motor control. **Confidence: moderate–high** that the gate is a convention.

## Findings

### Q1. Progression model (DUP / block / linear / RPE-autoregulated / VBT)

**Finding.** For trained lifters, (a) periodized > non-periodized for 1RM when volume is equated; (b) undulating ≥ linear, with the trained-subgroup estimate favouring undulating but imprecise; (c) block periodization is at least as effective as DUP in athletes and more efficient per unit volume; (d) RPE/RIR loading is at least as good as percentage loading and probably slightly better in trained lifters; (e) VBT is equivalent to percentage-based, not superior, and adds a measurement burden. **The program's DUP + RPE choice is defensible; it is not uniquely supported. Block periodization with RPE autoregulation would be equally defensible and slightly more efficient. Changing the model every ~2 blocks is weakly supported as a plateau-breaker.**

- Moesgaard et al. 2022, *Sports Med* — T1. Meta-analysis of volume-equated studies (35 studies), subgrouped by training status. Periodized vs non-periodized 1RM ES 0.31 [0.04, 0.57]. Undulating vs linear 1RM ES 0.31 [0.02, 0.61]; **trained subgroup ES 0.61 [0.00, 1.22]**; untrained ES 0.06 [−0.20, 0.31]. No hypertrophy differences. **Confidence: moderate** (trained CI touches zero; "trained" heterogeneous). https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1
- Williams et al. 2017, *Sports Med* — T1. 18 studies, mixed populations; periodized vs non-periodized 1RM d=0.43 → 0.23 after adjustment. **Confidence: moderate** for direction. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y
- Rhea et al. 2002, *JSCR* — T3 for this athlete. n=20 recreationally trained men, 12 wk, DUP > LP for bench/leg press 1RM. **Confidence: low transfer.** https://doi.org/10.1519/1533-4287(2002)016<0250:acolad>2.0.co;2
- Zourdos et al. 2016, *JSCR* — T2. n=18 college-aged male powerlifters, 6 wk, 3 d/wk. HPS order > HSP for bench 1RM (p≤0.05), greater total volume. **Confidence: moderate** (small n). Supports "heaviest session when freshest." https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001165
- Bartolomei et al. 2014, *JSCR* — T2. n=24 experienced resistance-trained men, 15 wk, 4 d/wk, volume-equated block vs traditional: block "possibly" (~60%) better for bench 1RM and power; no lower-body difference. **Confidence: low–moderate** (magnitude-based inference). https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000000366
- Painter et al. 2012, *IJSPP* — T2 (athletes). n=31 D-I track & field, 10 wk block vs DUP: no significant strength difference; block did it with significantly less volume load. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.7.2.161
- Helms et al. 2018, *Front Physiol* — T2. n=21 men ≥2 yr, squat ≥1.5×BW, bench ≥1.25×BW; 8-wk DUP, 3 d/wk, sets/reps matched, RPE vs %1RM. Squat +17.1 vs +13.9 kg (ES 0.50), bench +10.7 vs +9.6 kg (ES 0.28), NS. RPE group trained at higher relative intensity (bench 84.1% vs 78.7% 1RM). **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247
- Graham & Cleather 2021, *JSCR* — T2. n=31 resistance-trained men, 12 wk, 2 d/wk squat, RIR-autoregulated vs fixed %: back squat +10.8% vs +7.1%, front squat +11.7% vs +8.3%, significant. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000003164
- Hickmott et al. 2022, *Sports Med Open* — T1 (resistance-trained only, 15 studies). RPE vs %: MD 3.15 kg, SMD 0.30, p=0.06. VBT vs %: SMD 0.10, p=0.62. Velocity-loss ≤25% > >25% for 1RM (SMD 0.23, p=0.02). **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9
- Helms et al. 2017, *JSCR* — T2 (validation). n=12 powerlifters selecting loads to target RPE: mean absolute error 0.33 ± 0.28 RPE; more accurate close to failure. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002097
- Zourdos et al. 2016, *JSCR* — T4 (validation). n=29; experienced squatters' RPE tracks velocity r=−0.88. Validation only. https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001049
- Liao et al. 2021, *PLoS One* — T1. 6 studies, n=124, VBT vs PBT squat 1RM MD 3.0 kg [−3.6, 9.6], NS. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259790
- Zhang et al. 2023, *Int J Sports Med* — T1 (exploratory). 27 studies, 693 trained: 15–30% VL, 70–80% 1RM, 3–5 sets, 2–4 min rest; all periodization models effective; changing model ~every 9 wk may avoid plateau. **Confidence: low–moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2095-8254

### Q2. Frequency per lift

**Finding.** When weekly volume is equated, frequency has no consistent independent effect on strength in trained populations (Grgic 2018 subgroup; Cuthbert 2021; Colquhoun 2018). The newest and largest analysis (Pelland 2025) finds a modest independent frequency effect on strength with diminishing returns, from a dataset with low per-lift volume (8.1 fractional sets/wk) and mostly novel assessments, so learning is a plausible driver. The powerlifter MED work used 2–3 sessions per lift. **Two exposures per lift is the defensible choice. One heavy exposure plus accessories is a plausible *maintenance* dose, not the best *progression* dose. Three adds little unless volume rises. The "one heavy, one volume" split is a convention.**

- Grgic et al. 2018, *Sports Med* — T1. 22 studies. Frequency ES 0.74/0.82/0.93/1.08 for 1/2/3/4+ d/wk (p=0.003) but **volume-equated subgroup p=0.42**; authors: mostly untrained participants. **Confidence: high** that the headline effect is volume. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x
- Cuthbert et al. 2021, *Sports Med* — T1 (well-trained). 10 studies, volume-equated: upper g=0.088 (p=0.51), lower g=0.061 (p=0.65). Mostly 8–12RM protocols. **Confidence: moderate.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363540/
- Colquhoun et al. 2018, *JSCR* — T2. n=28 resistance-trained men, 6 wk, 3× vs 6× equated: bench +7.8 vs +8.8 kg; no group effects. https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002414
- Pelland et al. 2025, *Sports Med* — T1. 67 studies, n=2058, Bayesian multi-level meta-regression adjusted for training status. Frequency–strength: reciprocal best fit, 100% posterior probability of positive slope, diminishing returns; caveat: low average per-lift volume, learning effects. **Confidence: moderate** for a small effect; low for its size in an advanced lifter. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w
- Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021 — T2. Competitive powerlifters trained squat 2×, bench 3×, deadlift 1×/wk in the MED protocols that worked. https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655

### Q3. Set structure — top set + back-offs vs straight sets

**Finding.** No RCT directly compares top-set/back-off to straight sets in trained lifters; the practice is convention. The relevant trained-lifter evidence is indirect but consistent: (i) a daily heavy single alone does not drive progress, a single + back-off triples at 80% does (Androulakis-Korakakis 2021); (ii) training to a daily repetition maximum yields worse strength/RFD outcomes and more strain than submaximal relative-intensity sets (Carroll 2019); (iii) terminating sets at ≤25% velocity loss favours 1RM (Hickmott 2022); (iv) RIR has a negligible relationship with strength gain (Robinson 2024). **The stimulus is the accumulated sets at ~75–85% 1RM with 1–4 RIR; the top set is a probe. A structure that protects the back-off dose from a bad top set — fixed-reference back-offs or straight sets by RPE — is preferable to deriving back-offs from the day's top set. The risk the brief names (a fatigue-inflated single data point misguiding progression) is real and is amplified by the rising weekly RPE ceiling.**

- Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2021, *Front Sports Act Living* — T2. Study 3: n=16 male competitive powerlifters (Wilks ~347, ≥2 yr PL, ≥4 yr RT), 6 wk, 3 sessions/wk. MAX (single @ RPE 9–9.5 only): total +11.4 kg [0.8, 19.5], 6.3% probability of meaningful gain. MAX+boff (single + 2×3 @ 80% of the single): +33.7 kg [24.3, 44.1], 99.6%. Study 4: n=9, MAX+boff +26.8 kg vs AMRAP @70% +15.3 kg. Authors: ~3–6 working sets of 1–5 reps/wk per lift is a meaningful dose. **Confidence: moderate** (small n, ideal population, clean contrast). https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655
- Carroll et al. 2019, *IJSPP* — T2. n=15 well-trained men (IMTP ~4400 N), 10 wk, 3 d/wk; RM group (daily relative max) vs RISR group (percentages, submaximal): RISR better for jumps (g=0.76–1.07), RFD (g=0.89–1.25), scaled peak force (g=0.20); RM group had greater training strain at equal volume load. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2018-0045
- Hickmott et al. 2022 (above) — VL ≤25% superior for 1RM. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9
- Robinson et al. 2024, *Sports Med* — T1. Meta-regressions (67 strength studies, 55 hypertrophy), adjusted for load, volume-equating, duration, training status: RIR slope on strength includes null in all best-fit models; hypertrophy slope negative (closer → more growth). **Confidence: moderate** (exploratory; RIR estimated). https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2
- Helms et al. 2017 (above) — RPE accuracy worse further from failure. Relevant because the program anchors to an RPE 7.5 set.

### Q4. Intensity and effort distribution

**Finding.** For 1RM strength in trained men the evidence supports: most weekly work at 70–85% 1RM (Androulakis-Korakakis 2020), heavy rather than light loads (Schoenfeld 2017), sets stopped 1–4 reps short of failure with ≤25% velocity loss (Hickmott 2022; Robinson 2024), and a daily relative max avoided (Carroll 2019). The trained-lifter RPE trials that showed a benefit used rising RPE bands (5–7 → 8–10 over 8 wk) but averaged only ~RPE 7.2–7.3 across the block (Helms 2018). **The program's 7.5 → 9 top-set ramp is inside the tested envelope, but only the top set is at that RPE while the back-offs are far below it; the effective distribution is one hard set and three easy ones. The better distribution is: all working sets at RPE 7–8.5, top set as a probe, one RPE 9 test set per block.**

- Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020, *Sports Med* — T1 (resistance-trained men). Minimum effective dose: 1 set of 6–12 reps at ~70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/wk, high effort, 8–12 wk → "suboptimal yet significant" squat/bench 1RM gains. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0
- Schoenfeld et al. 2017, *JSCR* — T1. 21 studies, sets to failure, ≤60% vs >60% 1RM: 1RM gains significantly greater with high load; hypertrophy equal. **Confidence: high** for direction. https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000002200
- Robinson et al. 2024; Hickmott et al. 2022; Carroll et al. 2019; Helms et al. 2018 — as above.

### Q5. Rate of progress and stalls

**Finding.** The only large longitudinal dataset on rate of gain in strength athletes shows the strongest male quartile gaining ~0.10 kg/day on the powerlifting total (~37 kg/yr; ≈ 12 kg/yr per lift, with upper-body lifts typically the smaller share) versus ~0.21 kg/day for the weakest quartile — a ceiling effect (Latella 2020). Translating to this athlete: a 1.25–2.5 kg increment per lift per 5-week block is a good outcome; two blocks without an increment on a given lift is a stall, not noise. No trial tests micro-loading per se; the inference is arithmetic (a 2.5 kg jump on a 75 kg OHP is 3.3% ≈ 3 months of expected adaptation). For stalls, the trained-lifter evidence is: (i) rep or load progression are equally viable (Plotkin 2022); (ii) exercise variation costs nothing for 1RM and raises motivation (Baz-Valle 2019); (iii) changing the programming model every ~9 weeks may break plateaus (Zhang 2023, exploratory); (iv) a full week off costs strength short-term (Coleman 2024) — a stall is not, by itself, a reason to stop training the lift. Reset-and-rebuild is T5 practice with no trial evidence; it works by resetting the RPE calibration and reducing strain, which Carroll 2019 supports indirectly.

- Latella et al. 2020, *JSCR* — T1-adjacent observational (n=1897 competitive powerlifters, 15 yr). Strength gain/day: males 0.15 ± 0.44 kg·d⁻¹ overall; strongest quartile 0.102 vs weakest 0.211 kg·d⁻¹ (p=0.010); weak negative correlation between gain rate and baseline strength (r=−0.073). **Confidence: moderate** for the order of magnitude; competitors, not this athlete. https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000003657
- Plotkin et al. 2022, *PeerJ* — T2. n=43 with ≥1 yr lower-body training, 8 wk: load progression vs rep progression — strength slightly favoured LOAD (2.0 kg [−2.4, 7.8]), hypertrophy slightly favoured REPS; both viable. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14142
- Baz-Valle et al. 2019, *PLoS One* — T2. n=21 resistance-trained men, 8 wk, fixed vs randomized exercise selection: equal bench/squat 1RM gains; motivation ↑ with variation. **Confidence: moderate.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0226989
- Zhang et al. 2023 (above) — change model ~every 9 wk. **Confidence: low.**
- Coleman et al. 2024 (Q9) — week off costs strength.
- Micro-loading: **no peer-reviewed trial located** (searches returned only equipment marketing, T6). Recommendation below is inference.

### Q6. Weighted pull-up specifics

**Finding.** Load–velocity and load–%1RM relationships in the pull-up are near-perfect when load is computed as *system mass* (bodyweight + external). In 82 resistance-trained men, mean pull-up 1RM was 1.5 ± 0.34 × bodyweight of system mass (≈ +50% BW added); V1RM ≈ 0.20 m/s; the velocity–%1RM relationship was stable after 12 weeks and across strength levels. So estimated-1RM logic transfers to pull-ups *provided system mass is used*; percentages and e1RM computed on the belt load alone are invalid. Bodyweight fluctuation therefore changes the training load directly: a 2 kg bodyweight gain at 120 kg system mass is a 1.7% load increase with no change to the belt. Training studies in elite climbers show weighted-pull-up strength rising only +2–5% in 5 weeks — a realistic rate for a trained puller. Sensible rep ranges follow the barbell evidence (3–6 heavy, 5–8 volume); there is no pull-up-specific rep-range trial.

- Muñoz-López et al. 2017, *IJSPP* — T4 (acute, trained). n=82 resistance-trained men; individual load–velocity R² 0.975; 1RM system mass 1.5 ± 0.34 × BW; power-optimal load 71% 1RM. **Confidence: high** for the system-mass point. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2016-0657
- Sánchez-Moreno et al. 2017, *IJSPP* — T4. n=52 men; MPV–%1RM r=−0.96; V1RM 0.20 ± 0.05 m/s; relationships stable pre→post 12 wk (1RM +3.4%). **Confidence: high** for stability; moderate for transfer. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2016-0791
- Vigouroux & Devise 2024, *Bioengineering* — T2 (climbers). n=30 advanced-to-elite climbers, 5 wk, 2×/wk: max strength +2.2 to +5.0%; control unchanged. **Confidence: low–moderate** for transfer. https://doi.org/10.3390/bioengineering11010085
- Marques et al. 2025 (Q8) — general and individual load–velocity equations are reliable for 1RM estimation only at 70–90% 1RM; supports estimating e1RM from ≤6-rep sets, not from higher-rep sets.

### Q7. Overhead press specifics

**Finding.** Standing and seated barbell OHP produce similar deltoid EMG; dumbbells produce +11–15% deltoid EMG at ~10% lower 1RM (Saeterbakken & Fimland 2013, T4). There is **no outcome trial** comparing standing vs seated for 1RM progression, and **no trial** on OHP training frequency or on OHP–bench interference; I searched for both and found only practitioner content (T6). Inference from specificity: train the standing barbell lift as the lift. Inference from the incline EMG data (Q8): anterior deltoid contribution rises with bench angle (max at 60°), triceps is angle-insensitive, and 6RM incline loads run 18–21% below flat — so a 30° incline keeps the anterior-deltoid overlap with OHP as low as an incline can while still maximizing upper-pec involvement. Interference between OHP and incline in the same week is a fatigue-management problem, not an adaptation problem: both lifts are pressed twice weekly in the current program and the powerlifter evidence shows bench tolerates 3×/wk (Colquhoun 2018; MED protocols). The realistic cost is that the *second* press in each session is always fatigued; that is a sequencing choice.

- Saeterbakken & Fimland 2013, *JSCR* — T4. n=15 healthy men, EMG at 80% 1RM. Seated DB +11%, standing DB +15% deltoid EMG vs barbell; standing vs seated barbell not different; standing DB lowest 1RM. **Confidence: low** as a chronic guide. https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0b013e318276b873
- Rodríguez-Ridao et al. 2020; Saeterbakken et al. 2017 (Q8) — anterior deltoid and triceps overlap by angle.
- OHP frequency / OHP–incline interference: **no primary evidence located.** Recommendation is inference.

### Q8. Incline bench specifics

**Finding.** Upper (clavicular) pec EMG peaks at ~30°; anterior deltoid rises with angle and peaks at 60°; middle/lower pec peak at 0°; triceps is angle-insensitive (Rodríguez-Ridao 2020, n=30 trained). In national/international bench athletes, incline (wide grip) 6RM loads were 18.5–21.5% below flat, with 58–63% lower triceps EMG and higher biceps EMG, but no pec/deltoid differences (Saeterbakken 2017, n=12). In untrained men, 8 weeks of flat, incline or combined pressing produced equal gains in both flat and incline isometric strength (Chaves 2020) — high transfer in novices; in an advanced lifter specificity is expected to matter more. Load–velocity profiles differ between flat and incline (incline slower at every %1RM), and general/individual 1RM-prediction equations are acceptable for incline only from loads ≥70% 1RM (Marques 2025). **No incline-specific progression trial exists; incline should be progressed as its own lift with the barbell evidence above, at 30°, with e1RM estimated only from sets ≤6 reps.**

### Q9. Deloading

**Finding.** No controlled trial shows that a reduced-load deload week improves strength outcomes in trained lifters. One RCT in trained subjects shows a full *rest* week mid-block slightly reduces lower-body strength gains over 9 weeks with no effect on hypertrophy (Coleman 2024). In untrained subjects a reduced-volume/frequency deload at weeks 4 and 8 changed nothing (Pancar 2026). Practice and expert consensus converge on ~1 week of reduced stress every ~5–6 weeks, reducing sets, reps, load and effort while keeping frequency and exercises, triggered by plan and/or by stalls, soreness and joint aches (Rogerson 2024; Bell 2023). **A fixed every-5th-week deload at half sets, ~85% load, RPE 6 is consistent with practice and will not harm; it is not evidence-optimal. Given the modest per-lift volume here, a reactive trigger with a 6-week ceiling is at least as defensible. Do not deload by resting completely.**

- Coleman et al. 2024, *PeerJ* — T2. n=39 resistance-trained (29 M/10 F), 9-wk high-volume program; DELOAD abstained from RT for 1 week at midpoint. No differences in hypertrophy, endurance, power; **TRAD > DELOAD for isometric and dynamic lower-body strength.** Cessation, not a reduced-load week. **Confidence: moderate** that a full rest week costs some strength. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.16777
- Pancar et al. 2026, *Sci Rep* — T3. n=19 untrained men, within-subject: deload (1 session, 2 sets) at wk 4 and 8 vs continuous — no differences in thickness or 10RM. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-40612-5
- Rogerson et al. 2024, *Sports Med Open* — T5 (survey). n=246 competitive strength/physique athletes (8.2 yr RT): all deload; every 5.6 ± 2.3 wk, 6.4 ± 1.7 days; reduce sets, reps, load and effort (more RIR); keep frequency and exercises; pre-planned and/or reactive. **Confidence: high as description of practice.** https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y
- Bell et al. 2023, *Sports Med Open* — T5 (Delphi, 34→21 coaches). Consensus definition; deload may sit anywhere in the programme; purpose is fatigue mitigation and preparedness. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0

### Q10. Core for a strong lifter with an untrained trunk, under an adherence constraint

**Findings.**

1. *Does a strong lifter need a floor-based bracing-endurance phase before loaded work?* **No evidence says so.** The McGill "Big 3" evidence base is (a) clinical LBP populations and (b) mechanistic stiffness measures. In healthy men, 6 weeks of isometric holds increased *passive* trunk stiffness more than dynamic exercises; *active* stiffness did not change in any group (Lee & McGill 2015, n=24). Stiffness is a surrogate, not an injury outcome. No RCT gates loaded trunk work behind a motor-control phase in healthy lifters. Meanwhile, this athlete's decade of squats and deadlifts at ≥80% 1RM has already trained his lumbar erectors harder than side bridges or supermans do (Hamlyn 2007; Nuzzo 2008: erector activation in 80–100% 1RM squat/deadlift exceeds instability/floor exercises); the same studies show abdominal/oblique activation in squat/deadlift is *not* elevated — so what is genuinely untrained is the anterior and lateral trunk, not "bracing" per se. **Confidence: moderate–high** that the gate is a convention borrowed from rehab.

2. *Does trunk training prevent injury in lifters who also run, and does it care which exercises?* In **novice recreational runners** (the closest match to this athlete's running status), a supervised 24-week hip-and-core programme reduced LE injury incidence (HR 0.66) and substantial overuse injury prevalence by 52% versus stretching (Leppänen 2024, n=325, T2). I read the published programme: 8 exercise slots — (1) front plank family, (2) side plank / Copenhagen adductor, (3) pelvic lift (glute bridge), (4) miniband hip abduction, (5) hip flexor strength, (6) lunges, (7) hamstring (band deadlift, towel curl, Nordic), (8) side lunges; 2–3 sets each, resistance "instructed to feel heavy," progressed through four band levels and A–D variants; adherence 89%. **Six of eight slots are hip/leg strength that this athlete already trains beyond the trial's intensity; two are trunk holds.** The trial cannot tell us whether the plank slots contributed; the authors themselves attribute the effect to progressive resistance and supervision, contrasting a failed home-based unloaded programme (Toresdahl, 720 marathoners, no effect). A systematic review of core stability as an injury risk factor in athletes found core *strength* and neuromuscular control associated with LE injury, but evidence for core *endurance* (the plank quality) conflicting (De Blaiser 2018). In powerlifters/weightlifters the spine is a top injury site but no risk-factor or prevention trial exists (Aasa 2017). Trunk training in athletes improves strength, sprint and sport-specific performance (Saeterbakken 2022 meta, SMD 0.3–0.7), with >18 sessions and ≤30-min sessions most effective — exercise type was not a significant moderator. **Confidence: moderate** that hip+trunk strengthening reduces novice-runner overuse injury; **low** that it matters which trunk exercises deliver it.

3. *Is loaded flexion (cable crunch, hanging raises, decline sit-up) safe?* The caution derives from porcine in-vitro flexion-cycle work (Callaghan & McGill 2001 — cited by the debate literature; I did not open it) and has not been demonstrated in human training studies. The 2016 NSCA point/counterpoint concludes the risk–benefit verdict "remains elusive" and reserves avoidance for people with existing disc pathology (Schoenfeld & Kolber 2016, T5). EMG shows ab-wheel rollouts and hanging knee-ups produce the highest rectus/oblique activation of common exercises, with low lumbar paraspinal activity (Escamilla 2006, n=21, T4); integration exercises that also load shoulders/glutes activate trunk muscles more than isolation movements (Gottschall 2013, T4). **Confidence: moderate** that moderate-load, controlled flexion is safe in a healthy lifter; no evidence of benefit *or* harm from loaded flexion specifically on injury.

4. *Does core training help running?* Small T2/T3 evidence: 8 weeks of core training (3×/wk) improved plank endurance and running economy at high intensity in college athletes (Hung 2019, n=21). Direction plausible, magnitude small, population not this athlete.

**What is lost by skipping the mat.** Honestly: (a) the specific passive-stiffness effect of long isometric holds (T4 only), partly replaced by loaded isometrics (Pallof holds, suitcase carries, standing OHP) — no study has compared them; (b) low-cost, high-frequency practice (planks can be done daily) — irrelevant if he will not do them; (c) nothing with injury-outcome evidence behind it. **An adherent loaded programme beats a skipped mat programme by the entire effect size.**

## Recommendations for this athlete

Written to be applied directly. Loads are relative because current working loads were not supplied.

### A. Main-lift structure (all three lifts)

1. **Keep RPE autoregulation and within-week intensity variation (DUP).** Both are supported in trained lifters. Do not switch to VBT.
2. **Heavy day — invert the logic.**
   - Top set: **1 × 3–5 @ RPE 8** (2 RIR) in weeks 1–3. Record the e1RM. It is a probe.
   - Back-off sets: **3 × 4–6 @ 80–85% of the block-reference e1RM** (in RPE terms: 7–8). The block reference is the week-1 top-set e1RM; it updates upward only when a later top set beats it at or under RPE 8. **Do not derive back-offs from the day's top set.** If the top set came in ≥ RPE 9 unplanned, drop the back-offs to 80% and stop after 3 sets; do not skip them.
   - Week 4: **1 × 3 @ RPE 9** (or a single @ RPE 9 if preferred) as the block test, then back-offs as usual at 80%. The week-4 test sets the next block's reference; it does not, by itself, raise the working load.
3. **Volume day: 3–4 × 6–8 @ RPE 7–8 (≈ 72–80% 1RM)** for incline and OHP, **3 × 5–8** for pull-ups. Not 8–12; delt/arm hypertrophy is the accessories' job. Weekly direct sets per lift ≈ 7–8, inside the 3–9 window that worked in competitive powerlifters and past the point of steep diminishing returns without wasting recovery.
4. **Progression rule.** Load rises when *all back-off sets* on the heavy day reach the top of their range at ≤ RPE 8.5, and the volume day hits its range at ≤ RPE 8. Increments: **incline 2.5 kg (1.25 kg if a 2.5 kg jump has failed twice); OHP 1.25 kg; pull-up 1.25 kg on the belt.** Judge progress against a fixed RPE ceiling, not the rising weekly one.
5. **Expected rate.** One increment per lift per block is success. Two consecutive blocks without an increment on a lift = stall.
6. **Stall protocol (in order, one block each):** (i) check the running load and sleep before touching the lifting; (ii) swap the rep scheme on the heavy day (e.g. 5 × 3 @ 85% ↔ 3 × 6 @ 78%) — Plotkin 2022 shows rep- and load-progression are both viable; (iii) rotate the *volume-day* exercise to a close variant for one block (incline → close-grip incline or 15° incline; OHP → seated OHP or push press; pull-up → neutral-grip or chin-up) — Baz-Valle 2019 shows no strength cost and better motivation; (iv) reset the reference e1RM by 5% and rebuild with 1.25 kg steps; (v) if two lifts stall together, run one 4-week block-periodized mesocycle (wk 1–2: 4 × 6 @ 75–78%; wk 3–4: 5 × 3 @ 85–88%) before returning to DUP. Never resolve a stall with a full week off.
7. **Sequencing inside a session:** the lift with the heavy exposure goes first, always. (Whether a heavy press should follow a run day is the concurrent-training specialist's call; from the strength side, Zourdos 2016 says heaviest-when-freshest.)

### B. Lift-specific

- **Weighted pull-up.** Log bodyweight each pull-up day; compute e1RM and all percentages on **system mass** (bodyweight + belt load). Progress the belt load; when bodyweight moves ±1.5 kg, recompute the reference and adjust the belt so relative load is preserved. Estimate e1RM only from sets ≤ 6 reps. Ranges: heavy 3–5, volume 5–8.
- **Overhead press.** Train it standing (specificity; it is also loaded anti-extension trunk work). Use 1.25 kg increments as the default. Keep it as the *second* press on incline-heavy day and first on its own heavy day; count the other press's volume day as ~0.5 of a direct set when thinking about weekly dose.
- **Incline bench.** Fix the bench at **30°** (peak upper-pec EMG, lowest anterior-deltoid overlap with OHP that an incline allows). Expect 6RM loads ~20% below flat; do not compare to flat numbers. Estimate e1RM from ≤6-rep sets only.

### C. Deload

Keep the reduced-load design (half the sets, ~85% of working load, RPE ≤6, same exercises and frequency). **Trigger it reactively:** deload the following week if (a) heavy-day e1RM on any lift is ≥3% below the block reference two heavy sessions in a row, or (b) the week-4 test is missed on two lifts, or (c) joint pain or readiness is poor for a week — **with a hard ceiling of every 6th week** if no trigger fires. Never deload by stopping entirely.

### D. Core — loaded from day one, cable / machine / bench only

Two sessions per week, 10–15 min, at the end of the Pull day and after the easy run (a third optional on the long-run day). Three movement families per session, one exercise from each; rotate exercises between blocks for adherence. Progress by double progression (top of range at RPE 7–8 → add load); deload with the lifts.

| Family | Week 1–2 | Week 3–4 | Block 2+ (progressions) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anti-extension / flexion (rectus) | Cable crunch 2 × 10–15; or kneeling ab-wheel 2 × 6–10 (partial ROM) | 3 sets; ab-wheel to full kneeling ROM | Hanging knee raise → straight-leg raise → toes-to-bar; weighted decline sit-up 3 × 8–12; standing ab-wheel |
| Anti-rotation / rotation (obliques) | Pallof press 2 × 8–12/side (add a 3-s hold at full extension) | 3 sets; cable woodchop high-to-low 3 × 8–12/side | Half-kneeling Pallof; landmine rotation 3 × 6–10/side; low-to-high chop |
| Anti-lateral flexion (QL/obliques/adductors) | Suitcase carry 2 × 30 m/side or cable side bend 2 × 10–15 | 3 sets; Copenhagen plank on bench, knee level, 2 × 6–10/side | Copenhagen ankle level; heavier carries; single-arm overhead carry |

Notes: (i) the Pallof hold and the carries are the *isometric* component that the mat phase was meant to supply; keep the 2–3 s holds. (ii) Keep loaded flexion moderate and controlled — no ballistic end-range under heavy load — because the (weak) safety concern is about repeated loaded end-range flexion, not flexion as such. (iii) The hip half of the Leppänen programme (lunges, band RDL/Nordic, abduction, bridges) is covered by leg day; if the leg day is ever cut for running fatigue, keep one split-squat/lunge and one hip-hinge, because those — not planks — carried most of the injury-prevention programme's content. (iv) Sessions >18 total and ≤30 min are the pattern associated with performance effects (Saeterbakken 2022): the 10–15 min format is right.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions

- **Frequency.** Grgic 2018 and Cuthbert 2021 (no independent effect when volume-equated) vs Pelland 2025 (small positive independent effect). Both can be right: the Pelland effect is small, diminishing, and drawn from low-volume, novel-assessment studies. For a lifter already at 2×/lift the practical difference is nil.
- **Deloads.** Practice and consensus say every ~5–6 weeks; the only trained-lifter RCT (Coleman 2024) says a full rest week cost strength; no trial has tested a reduced-load week in trained lifters. If per-lift weekly volume were doubled, the case for a fixed deload would strengthen.
- **Block vs DUP.** The evidence cannot separate them in this athlete. The block option is listed in the stall protocol, not as the default.
- **Back-off anchor.** The Androulakis-Korakakis protocol *did* derive back-offs from the daily single — but from a RPE 9–9.5 single, which is the most accurately rated set a trained lifter performs (Helms 2017). Deriving from an RPE 7.5 set of 6 is a different thing. If the athlete's RPE calibration on submaximal sets turns out to be unusually good, the daily-anchor rule becomes less harmful; the "too light" problem remains regardless.
- **Incline transfer.** All incline-vs-flat training data are in untrained men. In an advanced lifter I am inferring that specificity matters more, so incline is trained as the incline.
- **OHP–incline interference.** No primary evidence. If progress on OHP lags incline for two blocks, the first move is to make OHP the first press in *both* sessions for a block (sacrificing incline volume-day quality), not to reduce pressing frequency.
- **Core.** Whether Leppänen's protection came from the hip work, the trunk holds, or the supervision/progression is unknown; the content analysis (6/8 hip) makes the hip explanation more likely. If the athlete developed low back symptoms, the recommendation would change toward McGill-style isometrics regardless of adherence preference — that is the population the mat evidence actually comes from.
- **Rate of gain.** Latella 2020 is competitive powerlifters on the three-lift total; the upper-body share and the drug-free status of that cohort are both unknown. The "one increment per block" expectation is inference.
