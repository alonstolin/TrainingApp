# Pull Day Vertical-Pull Redundancy — Research Findings

Narrow question commissioned by the lead agent: on Upper Pull (`lift:C`), heavy
weighted pull-up (T1, probe + 3 back-offs) is followed by chest-supported row,
then lat pulldown (3×10–12), reverse pec deck, incline DB curl and preacher
curl — ~17–18 sets total. Is the pulldown redundant given the pull-up, would a
pullover be a better slot-mate, or is a third option better. Research only; no
code or program files touched. Cross-checked against `research/hypertrophy.md`
(the accepted volume/fractional-counting research) and `research/SYNTHESIS.md`
§2.1–§2.3 so this stays consistent with what is already approved.

## Summary

- **Mechanistically, pull-up and lat pulldown recruit the lats almost
  identically.** Multiple small EMG studies (trained and mixed populations)
  find no significant difference in concentric latissimus dorsi amplitude
  between pull-ups and pulldown variants; the acute difference is that
  pull-ups add more elbow-flexor, spinal-erector and trunk-stabiliser
  recruitment (closed-chain, self-supported bodyweight), not more lat
  recruitment. The athlete's intuition is mechanistically correct. **Tier 4
  (EMG), moderate confidence in the equivalence itself, low confidence this
  settles a hypertrophy question** — no longitudinal RCT compares pull-up-only
  vs pulldown-only training on lat growth in any population.
- **A trained-lifter RCT on exercise variation itself (Baz-Valle 2019, n=21,
  8 wk) found no extra hypertrophy or strength from high variation over a
  fixed, volume-matched selection** — variation's only measured benefit was
  motivation. This weakens the case that keeping two vertical pulls (vs one)
  is doing anything extra for growth, but by the same logic it weakens the
  case that swapping one out will cost anything either.
- **A classic "pullover" — dumbbell or barbell, slight elbow bend, arcing over
  the chest — is not a lat-dominant exercise by EMG.** Three independent EMG
  studies (Marchetti & Uchida 2011; Lacio 2021; a 2022 MDPI comparison) agree
  the pectoralis major (and, in Lacio, the triceps long head) activate as much
  or more than the lats. If the athlete pictures the traditional pullover, it
  is a chest/triceps exercise wearing a lat exercise's name — a poor
  replacement for a "lat slot" and a bad idea generally, since it would inject
  unplanned direct pec volume into a muscle the program deliberately holds at
  maintenance.
- **A straight-arm cable pulldown/pullover (elbows fixed near-locked, pure
  shoulder extension) is the variant the evidence actually supports** for a
  lat-dominant, elbow-flexion-free option — the one EMG study that compared it
  directly against the barbell pullover (n=20, MDPI 2022) found it better for
  both lats and triceps long head. This is a single small study, not verified
  by me beyond the search-summary level (site blocked WebFetch), so treat as
  moderate-low confidence.
- **This is a second-order decision** in exactly the sense SYNTHESIS §2.3 uses
  the term for rear-delt exercise choice: no tier-1 or tier-2 evidence
  distinguishes these options on hypertrophy outcome. The strongest actionable
  levers here are session time (a real constraint per BRIEF) and the small,
  traceable knock-on effects on the biceps/rear-delt/triceps fractional totals
  already governed by §2.1 — not a hidden growth advantage for any one choice.

## Q1 — Is a second vertical pull redundant?

### (a) Evidence on exercise variation per muscle, in general

**Finding.** In trained men, varying exercise selection at matched volume and
effort produced statistically equivalent muscle thickness and strength gains
to a fixed selection; the only measured benefit of variation was subjective
motivation.

- Baz-Valle A, Rial-Vázquez J, Fariñas J, et al. 2019. "The effects of exercise
  variation in muscle thickness, maximal strength and motivation in
  resistance trained men." *PLoS ONE* 14(12):e0226989.
  https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0226989
  · RCT · **n = 21, resistance-trained men** · 8 weeks, push-pull program,
  3 sets × 6 exercises, 4×/week, fixed selection vs app-randomised selection
  each session at matched sets/effort · similar muscle-thickness and strength
  improvements between groups, variation group reported higher motivation ·
  **confidence moderate** (single trial, but directly in the target
  population and directly on point).

The counter-finding runs the other way and is in a different population:

- Fonseca RM, Roschel H, Tricoli V, et al. 2014. "Changes in exercises are
  more effective than in loading schemes to improve muscle strength." *J
  Strength Cond Res*. **n = 49, recreationally active but resistance-
  UNTRAINED males**, ~12 weeks, squat-pattern exercise variation vs constant
  squat · total quad CSA gain similar across groups, but the constant-exercise
  group failed to grow vastus medialis and rectus femoris while every varied
  group grew all four heads · read via secondary summary, not the primary PDF
  — **flag transfer problem explicitly: untrained subjects, single-joint
  variation (squat stance/depth), not cross-exercise-family variation, and I
  did not open the primary source.** **Confidence low** for any application
  here.

**Read for this athlete.** The trained-population trial (the one that
matters) found variation adds nothing measurable beyond volume-matched
consistency. The untrained finding — that varied exercise selection produces
more *complete regional* coverage than a single fixed exercise — is the kind
of claim that could support keeping two vertical pulls if the lats had
distinct, differentially-trainable regions the pull-up alone misses. That
question has *not* been answered for the lats (see the regional-EMG note
below); it has only been shown, in untrained people, for the quadriceps.

### (b) Evidence on pulldown vs pull-up specifically

**Finding.** Every source I could open or that was summarised in enough
detail agrees: pull-up and lat pulldown produce broadly equivalent
latissimus dorsi EMG amplitude; pull-ups add more elbow-flexor, spinal-
erector and trunk-stabiliser demand on top of an equivalent lat signal, not a
larger lat signal.

- A 2026 narrative EMG review — "Electromyographic Analysis of Latissimus
  Dorsi Activation During [Common Resistance Training Exercises]," *J Funct
  Morphol Kinesiol* 11(3):315, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13510307/
  — synthesised **23 studies, combined n ≈ 400+**, **training status ranged
  untrained to "≥5 y resistance-trained," mostly recreationally/resistance-
  trained** (opened and read directly). Verbatim: pull-ups/chin-ups show
  "broadly comparable LD EMG amplitude across grip configurations" across
  four studies (n≈84 combined); the review states no significant differences
  within latissimus dorsi, biceps brachii or posterior deltoid **between
  pull-ups and alternative pulling exercises** (i.e., including pulldown).
  Authors' own caveat, taken seriously: "acute EMG amplitude … cannot, by
  itself, determine which exercise produces superior long-term hypertrophy."
  **Confidence moderate** on the EMG equivalence itself; **low** on what it
  implies for growth, by the review's own stated limitation.
- Bauermeister MS. 1996 (Master's thesis, Western Michigan University),
  https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5534&context=masters_theses
  — EMG comparison of wide-grip pull-up vs lat pulldown; **concentric peak
  EMG magnitude was the same for pull-up and pulldown**, differences appeared
  in the eccentric phase and in timing, not concentric amplitude. Read only
  via search summary, not the primary thesis — **confidence low-moderate**,
  unverified primary, old (pre-2000), population/training status not
  established, cited here only because it independently converges with the
  2026 review.
- Grip-width EMG in the pulldown specifically: PMC12452428, **n = 40 males,
  minimum 5 years' resistance training** (opened and read directly), seven
  grip/orientation variants at 70% RM — **no significant differences in
  latissimus dorsi activation across any of the seven variants**; only the
  posterior deltoid differed (trunk-inclined wide grip > flat wide/neutral
  grip). This tells you grip and handle choice inside the pulldown family
  barely move the needle on the lats either — the redundancy argument would
  apply just as much to swapping pulldown grips as to swapping pulldown for
  something else. **Confidence moderate**, trained population, directly on
  point, single study.

**No longitudinal training study exists comparing pull-up-only vs pulldown-
only (or pull-up+pulldown vs pull-up-only) programmes on lat hypertrophy, in
any training-status population.** I searched specifically for this and found
none. This is the load-bearing gap: everything above is acute/mechanistic
(tier 4), and the evidence standards in the brief say tier-4 evidence is
suggestive, not conclusive, on its own.

### (c) Inference

Given (a) — variation itself adds nothing measurable in trained lifters at
matched volume — and (b) — pull-up and pulldown load the lats through
essentially the same EMG signal — the honest read is: **the second vertical
pull is not adding a *different* stimulus to the lats that the first one
doesn't already provide, but it also isn't wasted.** It is a full, controllable,
isolated direct set (no grip-strength/bodyweight ceiling, no compounding
systemic fatigue from spinal erectors and forearms the way another heavy
pull-up set would) that the program can dose at a specific RIR and rep range
the fatigued end of a heavy pull-up set cannot reliably hit. That is a real,
if modest, practical advantage of the *pulldown format* over "just do more
pull-ups" — it does not argue for having *both* a pull-up and a pulldown in
the same session specifically. This is squarely the same category of
decision SYNTHESIS §2.3 already reached for rear-delt exercise selection:
**second-order, not zero-order.** I am not going to manufacture a hypertrophy
distinction the evidence doesn't support: on the specific question "does the
lat pulldown add a distinct growth stimulus the pull-up doesn't," the honest
answer is *no strong evidence either way, plausibly not much*.

## Q2 — Would a pullover be a better slot-mate?

**The answer depends entirely on which pullover, and the evidence points in
opposite directions for the two variants people call by that name.**

### Bent-arm / classic dumbbell or barbell pullover — not a good replacement

Three independent EMG studies agree this variant is pec-dominant (and, per
one, triceps-long-head-dominant), not lat-dominant:

- Marchetti PH, Uchida MC. 2011. "Effects of the pullover exercise on the
  pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi muscles as evaluated by EMG." *J
  Appl Biomech* 27(4):380–4. https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jab/27/4/article-p380.xml
  · **n = 8 healthy males**, training status not stated · barbell pullover,
  1×10 @ 30% body weight · pectoralis major activation exceeded latissimus
  dorsi, "the higher activation depended on the external force lever arm" ·
  read via secondary summary — **confidence low-moderate**, small n, old,
  population unclear.
- Lacio ML, Teixeira JD, Vieira JG, et al. 2021. "Electromyographic
  Activation of Pectoralis Major and Triceps Brachii during Dumbbell
  Pullover." *JEPonline* 24(4):1–11. https://www.asep.org/asep/asep/JEPonlineAUGUST2021_Lacio_Vieira.pdf
  · **opened and read directly (full PDF)** · crossover RCT · **n = 13
  males, minimum 12 months' RT** · dumbbell pullover at 90% 10RM, elbow
  extended vs elbow flexed · did **not measure the lats at all** (measured
  pec sternal, pec clavicular, triceps long head only) · triceps long head
  EMG (54–56% RMS) exceeded pec clavicular fibres in both elbow positions
  (P<0.05) and exceeded pec sternal fibres with elbow flexion (P=0.002); no
  interaction effect of elbow position (P=0.312) · the introduction cites
  Marchetti & Uchida (2011) and Borges et al. finding PM > LD in the
  pullover, and Campos et al. finding **no difference** between PM, LD,
  triceps long head, deltoid and serratus in yet another pullover variant ·
  **confidence moderate** on the triceps-long-head activation (directly
  measured, trained-adjacent population, clean design); **the lat question
  is not settled by this study** — it wasn't tested.
- MDPI 2022 (*Applied Sciences* 12(21):11138), "Comparison of
  Electromyographic Activity during Barbell Pullover and Straight Arm
  Pulldown Exercises" · **n = 20, "healthy and physically active"** (not
  clearly resistance-trained) · barbell pullover vs cable straight-arm
  pulldown, two biacromial-width grips · pullover showed **higher EMG than
  straight-arm pulldown in every muscle except the latissimus dorsi and
  triceps brachii** — for those two, the straight-arm pulldown won ·
  **WebFetch was blocked (403) on this source; findings are from a search-
  engine summary only, not verified against the primary text — treat as
  moderate-low confidence and flag that I could not open it.**

**Read for this athlete.** A bent-arm dumbbell/barbell pullover is best
described as an accessory chest-and-triceps-long-head movement, not a lat
movement. Putting it in the "lat slot" on Pull day would (a) not clearly
solve the redundancy the athlete is worried about, since it may recruit the
lats *less* than the pulldown it replaced, and (b) quietly add direct
pectoral volume to a muscle the program has deliberately parked at
maintenance (BRIEF: "everything else is maintained"), which nothing in
SYNTHESIS accounts for. **Do not use this variant here.**

### Straight-arm cable pulldown/pullover — the variant that fits the brief

This is elbows fixed near-locked throughout, pulling a cable from overhead
down to the thighs (or the mirror-image dumbbell/cable "pullover" with
minimal elbow bend) — pure shoulder extension, no meaningful elbow flexion.
It is the variant the MDPI comparison above found to beat the barbell
pullover for both lats and triceps long head, and the one that actually
answers what the athlete asked for:

- **Resistance profile / lengthened position.** The lats are most lengthened
  with the arm overhead — the top of this movement's range. A cable or
  machine can be set up to keep meaningful tension there; a gravity-loaded
  dumbbell physically cannot, because with the arm near-vertical the
  moment arm (and therefore the torque a hanging weight produces) collapses
  toward zero exactly at the position you want loaded. **This is my own
  biomechanical inference (lever-arm reasoning), not a cited trial result —
  state it as such.** It is consistent with the general "longer muscle
  length training produces more hypertrophy" literature (a 2025 systematic
  review, cited in `research/hypertrophy.md` §3, found small-to-moderate
  favouring effects at longer muscle lengths), but **that literature has not
  been tested on the lats specifically** — it is built on elbow flexors,
  elbow extensors, quadriceps and calves. Applying it to the lats here is an
  extrapolation across muscle groups, exactly the kind of move the brief
  asks me to flag rather than launder into "the evidence shows." **Confidence
  low-moderate**, inference stacked on an evidence base that does not include
  this muscle.
- **Removing elbow flexion.** Straight-arm removes elbow flexion entirely, so
  it stops competing with, or double-counting into, the biceps — which
  matters because the athlete already has 5 direct biceps sets later in this
  same session (incline DB curl + preacher curl) plus more on Lower day, and
  `research/hypertrophy.md` §2 already counts rows+pulldown together as
  worth **+3 fractional sets/week to biceps** via elbow-flexor synergism. A
  straight-arm variant would not contribute that credit — see Q4 for the
  arithmetic. Biceps is nowhere near its floor, so losing that credit costs
  nothing.
- **Triceps long head bonus.** The long head of triceps crosses the shoulder
  and assists shoulder extension — the Lacio 2021 result (triceps long head
  EMG exceeding pec clavicular fibres, and matching or exceeding pec sternal
  fibres, in a mechanically similar movement) makes it plausible a
  straight-arm cable version would add a modest, previously-absent indirect
  credit toward the *triceps* fractional total — a priority muscle that
  currently gets **zero** contribution from anything on this day. This is
  the one genuinely novel upside relative to the pulldown it would replace.
  **Confidence low-moderate**: inferred from an EMG study on a related but
  not identical exercise variant, not measured on the straight-arm cable
  version itself, and not a hypertrophy outcome.

### Cable vs dumbbell vs machine, if the distinction is supported

For lateral raises — the closest tested analogue in this athlete's own
program — cable and dumbbell were found **equivalent** in trained lifters
(Wolf/Schoenfeld 2025, n=24, 7.1 y trained, already cited at SYNTHESIS §2.3
and `hypertrophy.md`). By that precedent, I would *not* expect a strong
outcome difference between cable, dumbbell or machine pullover either, if all
three are loaded near failure through a comparable range. **But** the
pullover's bottom/lengthened position is mechanically different from a
lateral raise's arc in a way that specifically penalises free-weight loading
right where you want tension (see above) — a difference that doesn't apply to
the lateral raise. So: **cable or machine over dumbbell for this specific
exercise, on biomechanical grounds, not because any trial tested it.**
Machine and cable are likely interchangeable with each other. **This entire
paragraph is inference; no trial compares implements for the pullover.**

## Q3 — Or is a third option better?

Four alternatives considered, each against the athlete's own stated
priorities (shoulders and arms; back at maintenance; ~60–75 min sessions;
this day already at ~17–18 sets):

1. **A second horizontal pull** (e.g., single-arm cable row) instead of a
   second vertical pull. Diversifies pulling plane, adds a small amount of
   extra posterior-delt/rhomboid synergist work. **Does not solve a real
   gap** — rear delts already get direct work from reverse pec deck same day
   and face pull on Sunday (per `hypertrophy.md` §3, rear delts sit at ~9
   fractional sets/week at baseline before any of this, inside the 10–14
   band once the day's other work is counted); more indirect rear-delt
   credit here is marginal. No meta-analytic or RCT evidence that two
   horizontal pulls beat one horizontal + one vertical for back or rear-delt
   hypertrophy at matched volume — this is inference, and a weak case.
2. **A lat-focused unilateral option** (single-arm/kneeling lat pulldown).
   The only relevant meta-analysis found **no significant difference in
   muscle hypertrophy between unilateral and bilateral training**: "Comparison
   of Muscle Growth and Dynamic Strength Adaptations Induced by Unilateral
   and Bilateral Resistance Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis,"
   *Sports Medicine* 2024, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02169-z
   · systematic review + meta-analysis · population mixed across included
   trials, mostly general trained samples (not specifically reported by
   training status in the summary I could access — **confidence moderate**,
   read via search summary/abstract-level detail, not the full text).
   Unilateral work has plausible per-side-attention and stabiliser benefits
   but **no hypertrophy evidence advantage**, and it typically takes longer
   per set (two sides) — actively works against the time constraint. **Not
   recommended** on the current evidence.
3. **A rear-delt/upper-back movement** in this slot. Rejected for the same
   reason as (1): rear delts are already covered by reverse pec deck (same
   day) and face pull (Sunday); adding a third rear-delt-biased movement here
   doesn't fill a demonstrated gap, it just adds more rear-delt volume to a
   muscle already inside its band, at the cost of session time.
4. **Drop the slot; leave back volume at row + pull-up.** The plain reading
   of the evidence above (Q1) is that the marginal lat-specific stimulus from
   the third pulling movement in this session is small and unproven. Back is
   explicitly not a priority muscle (BRIEF: "everything else is maintained"),
   and SYNTHESIS §2.4 already applies exactly this logic to legs — cutting to
   roughly a third of "building" volume on the reasoning that a maintenance
   muscle doesn't need a growth dose (Spiering 2021 narrative review; Bickel
   2011, **untrained subjects**, detraining study — SYNTHESIS itself flags
   this evidence as thin and I am extending the same logic to back by
   analogy, not new data). Dropping the slot saves 3 working sets plus their
   rest intervals — at the day's rest periods (120 s/set) that's roughly
   6–8 minutes of session time, directly against the BRIEF's 60–75 minute
   constraint on a day that is currently one of the longest in the week.

## Q4 — Effect on weekly back volume, and whether it stays inside §2.1

**Back/lats are not one of the four priority muscles in SYNTHESIS §2.1.**
§2.1's fractional band (12–16 side delts, 10–14 rear delts, 12–16 triceps,
12–16 biceps) governs side delts, rear delts, triceps and biceps only — it
does not set a target for back. So strictly, there is no §2.1 band for back
volume to stay inside or fall outside of; the real question is (i) whether
back volume stays comfortably above a maintenance floor, and (ii) what the
knock-on effect is on the *priority* muscles that this slot indirectly
credits — biceps and rear delts, per `hypertrophy.md`'s own fractional count
("rows + pulldown ~6 → +3 to biceps, +3 to rear delts").

Baseline, as the athlete's own tally states: **4 heavy pull-up + 3 pull-up
volume + 3 row + 3 pulldown ≈ 13 direct back/lat sets/week.**

- **Swap pulldown → straight-arm cable pullover (Q2's recommended variant).**
  Direct back-adjacent sets unchanged at 13/week. Indirect credit shifts:
  the elbow-flexor-synergist contribution from this slot to biceps drops
  (straight-arm removes elbow flexion), taking the row+pulldown-slot biceps
  credit from ~+3 down to roughly +1.5 (row alone); biceps' weekly fractional
  total (baseline ~14–15 per `hypertrophy.md`) would fall to roughly ~13,
  still inside 12–16. Rear-delt credit is plausibly little changed (row and
  the straight-arm posterior-delt synergism both contribute) — call it flat
  to −0.5, immaterial against the 10–14 band, which is dominated by direct
  reverse-pec-deck and face-pull sets elsewhere. A new, previously-absent
  triceps credit of roughly +1–1.5 fractional appears (this day currently
  contributes zero to triceps); weekly triceps total (baseline ~14–15) would
  rise toward the top of the 12–16 band — worth a note to not also add
  triceps accessory volume elsewhere without checking the total.
- **Drop the slot entirely.** Direct back/lat sets fall from 13 to
  **10/week** (a ~23% cut, still well above any published minimum-effective
  or maintenance figure I found — `hypertrophy.md`'s own minimum-effective
  dose for a priority muscle under Pelland 2025 is 4 fractional sets/week,
  and back is not even a priority muscle). Biceps credit from this day falls
  from ~+3 (row+pulldown) to ~+1.5 (row only); combined with the unchanged
  pull-up credit, weekly biceps fractional total falls from ~14–15 to
  roughly ~13, still inside 12–16. Rear-delt credit from this slot falls
  from ~+3 to ~+1.5; weekly rear-delt total (baseline ~9 fractional before
  this day's direct reverse-pec-deck sets are added, per `hypertrophy.md`)
  moves down by roughly 1.5 fractional sets — check this against the full
  weekly rear-delt total before acting; reverse pec deck (this day) and face
  pull (Sunday) are unaffected and are the larger direct contributors, so I
  expect the total stays inside 10–14, but I have not recomputed the full
  weekly rear-delt ledger here — that is outside this narrow question's
  scope and belongs to whoever owns §2.1's running totals.

**Neither option threatens the §2.1 bands for biceps or rear delts on the
numbers above.** Back/lat volume stays well above maintenance in both cases.

## Recommendation

This is a second-order decision: no tier-1 or tier-2 evidence distinguishes
"keep the pulldown," "swap it for a straight-arm cable pullover," or "drop
the slot" on hypertrophy outcome, and I am not going to manufacture a
distinction the literature doesn't support. My actual pick, in order: **drop
the lat-pulldown slot.** Back is explicitly a maintenance muscle for this
athlete, 13 direct sets/week was already generous and 10 remains well above
any maintenance floor in the literature, the EMG evidence says pull-up and
pulldown are training the lats through essentially the same mechanism so the
marginal stimulus lost is small, and dropping the slot recovers real session
time against the BRIEF's 60–75 minute constraint on a day that currently runs
long. If the athlete would rather keep six exercises, or wants the small
speculative triceps-long-head/lengthened-lat bonus, **swap lat pulldown for a
straight-arm cable pulldown/pullover (elbows fixed, 3×10–15, RPE 8–9, last
set to RPE 10)** — not a bent-arm dumbbell or barbell pullover, which the EMG
evidence says is a chest movement, not a lat one. Either choice is
evidence-consistent; "change nothing" is also defensible if the athlete
simply likes the exercise and the extra 6–8 minutes doesn't bother him — the
evidence does not compel a change, it only shows the change costs little and
buys a little time back.

## Confidence

**Moderate on the diagnosis (pull-up and pulldown are mechanistically
near-redundant for the lats), low-moderate on the specific recommendation.**
The diagnosis rests on several small, independent EMG studies that converge
(pull-up ≈ pulldown; bent-arm pullover ≈ chest, not lats; straight-arm
pulldown > bent-arm pullover for lats) — consistent convergence across
independent small studies is worth more than any one of them alone, which is
why I rate it moderate rather than low. But every piece of this specific
question is tier-4 (acute EMG) or tier-5-adjacent inference by the brief's
own hierarchy: there is no trained-lifter RCT, and no RCT at all, that trains
people with pull-up-only vs pull-up-plus-pulldown vs pull-up-plus-straight-
arm-pullover and measures lat growth. If such a trial existed and disagreed
with the EMG-based reasoning here, it would win. Absent it, this is the
honest ceiling of what can be said.
