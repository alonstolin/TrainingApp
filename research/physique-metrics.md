# Physique Assessment — Research Findings (Mode A: Criteria)

**A note on evidence access, up front, because it bears on how much to trust everything below.** Of the sources cited here, I successfully opened and read four primary documents in full (the ACL/LSI critical-analysis paper, the muscle-specific strength-asymmetry study, the tape-measure reliability study, and the BJJ asymmetry pilot). For a larger number of claims — ISAK's technical-error figures, the original Grindem 2016 LSI paper, the Maisey et al. 1999 *Lancet* attractiveness paper, muscle-pump magnitude/duration, self-measured-circumference validity, diurnal circumference variation, photogrammetric-posture software validation, and bodybuilding judging-criteria literature — attempts to open the primary source failed (PDF binary parsing, a cookie/auth redirect loop, or a journal paywall), and what follows rests on search-engine-synthesized summaries of those sources, not a verified read. I mark every such claim **[secondary, unverified primary]** and weight it accordingly. Where I found nothing at all after a real search — notably, visual assessment of *muscle size* against an imaging gold standard, and inter-rater reliability of bodybuilding judging — I say so; absence of evidence after 25+ searches is itself the finding, not a gap to paper over.

## Summary

- **"Lagging body part" has no validated operational definition.** No study was found — after specifically searching for one — that tests whether a human eye (trained or not) can correctly identify an under-developed muscle from a photograph against an imaging ground truth (DXA/ultrasound/MRI). The concept collapses into two things that already exist in this programme and don't need a photo: (a) *priority muscle* — side delts, rear delts, biceps, triceps, already volume-banded in SYNTHESIS §2.1 — and (b) *under its own volume band*, which is a training-log fact, not a visual one.
- **No validated protocol exists for assessing muscle-size asymmetry from photographs**, in bodybuilding, physiotherapy, or anywhere else I could find. The nearest real frameworks (Limb Symmetry Index, ISAK anthropometry) are built for different tissues, different questions, and in LSI's case a population that should not be assumed to transfer.
- **The Limb Symmetry Index's 90% threshold is a consensus convention, not a validated one** — the one paper I read that interrogated it directly found it unassociated with its own outcome in a 233-athlete cohort, and it was derived entirely in post-surgical ACL populations measuring strength/hop performance, not girth, in people under 30 recovering from knee reconstruction. Applying a "10% difference = problem" rule to this athlete's arm tape would be borrowing a number from the wrong tissue, the wrong outcome, and the wrong population.
- **Some single-digit-percent side-to-side difference is normal and expected in uninjured, trained people.** The best data found: 3–6% average dominant-side strength advantage at the elbow in healthy active adults, with >65% of individuals asymmetric by at least one measure beyond a smallest-worthwhile-change threshold; ~5% DXA-measured lean-mass asymmetry in male combat athletes. None of this is circumference data on a bilateral lifter, so treat the "~3–7% is unremarkable" convention as an **inference**, not a measured fact for this exact case.
- **The tape protocol in `coach/PHYSIQUE.md` is broadly right in landmark choice and wrong in what it asks the athlete to conclude.** It needs: always three reps (not two-then-maybe-three), a fixed statistic (median), and — most importantly — an explicit statement that a *single week's* arm or calf change is almost certainly smaller than measurement error for an advanced lifter and should not be reviewed weekly at all.
- **Least-bad weekly-change threshold:** treat anything under **~1 cm** change in upper-arm circumference (relaxed or flexed), and **~1.3–1.5 cm** for calf, as indistinguishable from noise, using the closest reliability study found as a floor and adjusting upward for self-measurement with an uncalibrated cloth tape. This is a convention, explicitly flagged as such, not a number derived from this athlete's own technique.
- **For left-vs-right specifically, same-day repeat spread is the better error bar than any literature number** — three reps per side, same sitting, same tape; if the gap between sides is no bigger than the spread within one side, it isn't a finding.
- **The dated capture audits and anything resting on the athlete's own logged data live in `coach/reports/`, which is gitignored** — this repo is public. This file holds the criteria; the application of them to a given week's frames, tape and review report is on that side of the line.

## Where the provisional protocol (`coach/PHYSIQUE.md`) is wrong, unsupported, or should change

1. **"Measure twice... if they disagree by more than a couple of mm, measure a third"** — conditional third measurement produces an inconsistent N across sites and sessions, and invites the athlete to stop as soon as two numbers happen to agree, which is not the same as establishing real precision. **Change to: always three independent measurements, full tape removal and reapplication between each, record all three, track the median.** This is a design recommendation, not evidence-backed; flagged as least-bad convention.
2. **No stated threshold for "a weekly change smaller than [measurement error] means nothing."** The file names the principle but not the number. See Finding 3 below for the number, and the more important point: at an advanced lifter's growth rate, *no single week* should be expected to clear that number, so the comparison cadence that matters is block-to-block, not week-to-week, even though taking the measurement weekly (cheap) is still worth doing to build a trend line.
3. **The protocol implies the frames can speak to "what is lagging."** They cannot, for the reasons in Finding 1. The file should say explicitly that the camera answers "what changed enough to see with the naked eye" and "is there a gross, unmissable asymmetry," not "which muscle is behind by how much."
4. **"Back lat spread... hold it the same way every week"** is good instruction, but worth restating with the specific technique named (hands on hips, elbows pulled forward, not a rear double-biceps fist pose), because a rear double-biceps is an easy and silent substitution for it, and the two display different muscles.
5. **No instruction to avoid measuring/shooting within ~48h of training the muscle being assessed.** Exercise-induced muscle damage and the associated fluid shift can inflate girth for 24–72h post-session (general EIMD mechanism, well established in the exercise-physiology literature; I am not citing a specific girth-magnitude paper here, so treat this as a mechanistically-grounded caution, not a quantified correction). Recommend fixing capture day to be the same weekday every week, chosen to be ≥48h removed from direct arm/calf training.
6. **No floor/vertical reference actually reaches the photo.** The protocol asks for floor tape marks and a vertical in shot, but asking is not enough: a frame cropped at the waist contains neither, and a vertical on the far wall establishes only that the camera was square to the *wall*, not that the subject was square to the camera. The instruction is right; it needs a concrete framing requirement (full body in shot, and a reference in the subject’s own depth plane).

## Findings

### 1. By what metric is a muscle "lagging"?

**Finding: there is no validated way to identify an under-developed muscle from a photograph, and the concept does not survive contact with what this programme can already measure.**

- **Visual/photographic judgment of muscle size against a ground truth (DXA/ultrasound/MRI): no study found.** I ran five separate searches aimed at this specific question (visual estimation of hypertrophy from photos vs. imaging; trainers/judges estimating muscle mass; bodybuilding judge reliability). Every hit was either ultrasound-vs-DXA (instrument vs. instrument, not eye vs. instrument) or body-*fat* estimation from photos, not muscle size. The closest thing found was photograph-based body-fat estimation: a 2018 algorithmic (not human-visual) method correlating r = 0.80–0.87 with DXA body-fat% (Wolfe et al., *PLOS ONE* 2018) **[secondary, unverified primary — I did not open this paper directly]**, and a separate, uncited claim that naive human visual %BF estimates correlate r = 0.56–0.83 with criterion measures **[secondary, unverified primary, source chain unclear]**. Neither is about muscle size, and both are about fat, which is a separate question from "which muscle is lagging." Confidence that visual muscle-lagging judgment is unvalidated: **high** (absence after a real search, on a question specific enough that a positive result would have been easy to find).
- **Bodybuilding/physique judging criteria (symmetry, proportion, "classic" shoulder-to-waist ratios) rest on organisational convention and a separate, narrower body of attractiveness-perception research, not on a validated judging methodology.** Federation rulebooks (IFBB, WFF, ANBF, BNBF) define symmetry as side-to-side equality and proportion as inter-muscle balance **[tier 6, organisational convention]**, but no inter-rater reliability or validity study of the judging process itself turned up in search, despite looking specifically for one. The nearest related data point is from a different domain entirely: an exploratory inter/intra-rater reliability study of expert assessors scoring *movement quality* from body-shape visualizations found only slight-to-fair agreement (κ ≈ 0.35–0.45) even among trained raters (Senington et al., *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation* 2024, exploratory design) — suggestive that expert visual scoring of bodies is not inherently high-agreement, but the task (movement, not static size) and population don't transfer cleanly; **confidence low, cited as caution only.**
- **Shoulder-to-waist ratio specifically traces to Maisey, Vale, Cornelissen & Tovée (1999, *The Lancet*)**, which reportedly found waist-to-chest ratio the principal predictor of female raters' attractiveness scores of male photographs, accounting for a majority of rated variance **[secondary, unverified primary — I could not extract readable text from the PDF in two attempts; numbers here are search-engine paraphrase, not a verified read]**. Two things matter even if that number holds up: first, this is an *attractiveness-to-strangers* outcome, not a muscular-development or training-target outcome — a ratio that predicts a rating from onlookers says nothing about whether a muscle is under-trained relative to its own potential. Second, the popular "1.618 golden ratio / Adonis Index" framing is a marketing overlay on this literature, not what the paper concluded (a continuous relationship, not a single target ratio) **[tier 6 commentary, noted only to flag the gap between the research and the popular convention]**.
- **"Lagging body part" collapses into two already-measurable things, neither of which needs a photo:**
  1. *Priority muscle by programme design* — side delts, rear delts, biceps, triceps, with fractional-set bands already set in SYNTHESIS §2.1 (12–16, 10–14, 12–16, 12–16 respectively). This is a programme decision already made on cited evidence (Pelland 2025, tier 1), not something a photograph should re-litigate.
  2. *Below its own volume band* — a fact about the training log (direct/fractional sets actually performed vs. the band), which is exactly what `tools/review.mjs` now computes (per `coach/DECISIONS.md`, 2026-09-30 entry). Whether a given report is a *usable* source is a separate question answered in the dated capture audit; a report built on too few weeks, incomplete adherence or missing RPE can show "under band" rows that are logging artifacts rather than programming shortfalls. Either way the principle holds: a volume-band shortfall is a log fact, not a photo fact.
  A third category — "this muscle looks small relative to the others, independent of volume received" — is the one a photo might seem to answer, and it is precisely the one with no validation behind it. The regional-hypertrophy literature already assembled elsewhere in this project (SYNTHESIS §2.3: Varović meta-analysis, regional effects SMD 0.04–0.09, tier 1, moderate-high confidence) shows that even *within* a muscle, exercise-selection effects on shape are trivial — reinforcing that "this part looks behind" is usually not an actionable training signal even when the visual impression is real.
  - **Expert opinion (tier 5):** Eric Helms — natural pro bodybuilder and sports scientist — is reported to argue against defaulting to "add volume to the lagging muscle," on the grounds that muscle-specific responsiveness varies and the opportunity cost (volume taken from a more-responsive muscle) can produce *less* total growth than leaving the allocation alone **[secondary, unverified primary — paraphrase of an interview/article, not a primary citable passage]**. This is consistent with, not independent evidence for, the diminishing-returns/no-plateau volume-response shape already established in SYNTHESIS §2.1 (Pelland 2025, tier 1).

**Conclusion:** for an advanced natural lifter with a programme that already fractionally counts volume per priority muscle, "lagging body part" is not a coherent additional diagnostic category. The only coherent uses of a photograph here are (a) gross, naive-eye-obvious underdevelopment or injury-level asymmetry that would be visible to anyone, not a fine-grained ranking, and (b) a sanity check against what the log already says, not a replacement for it.

### 2. Is there a validated protocol for assessing asymmetry?

**Finding: no. The nearest frameworks are real, but every one of them is built for a different tissue, a different outcome, or a different population than "is this lifter's arm or lat symmetrical."**

- **Limb Symmetry Index (LSI), ACL rehabilitation.** Real, widely used, with a conventional 90% cutoff for hop-test and isokinetic quadriceps-strength batteries before return to sport. I read the primary critique directly (Losciale et al.-type critical analysis, *accessed via* PMC11874420, n = 233 ACL-reconstructed athletes, mean age 20.5, Swedish Project ACL registry, 51% women): the paper states plainly that the 90% cutoff "has been accepted through consensus statements and expert opinions but [has] not previously been validated," and in this cohort the 90% threshold showed **no significant association** with the safe-return-to-sport outcome it is meant to predict. **Confidence: moderate-high that the 90% figure is unvalidated as stated; population is post-surgical ACL patients aged 15–30, which is a hard ceiling on how far this transfers to a healthy 10-year-trained lifter.**
  - The foundational positive result behind the convention (Grindem et al. 2016: LSI <90% associated with 33% vs. 13% second-ACL-injury rate) **[secondary, unverified primary — I did not open this paper]** is about second-injury risk in a reconstructed knee, using strength and hop performance, not muscle size, and not an uninjured population.
  - A frequently repeated claim that **only ~24% of uninjured athletes "pass" a 90% LSI battery** would, if true, be the single most important fact here — it would mean the "normal" threshold fails most people who have nothing wrong with them. I could not trace this to a verifiable primary source; it appears only in secondary blog commentary **[secondary, unverified primary, low confidence — flagged rather than dropped, because if true it is decisive against borrowing 90%/10% as a threshold, and even as an unverified claim it is a reason for caution]**.
  - **Transfer cost to this athlete, stated plainly:** LSI's evidence base is reconstructed knees, strength/hop performance, ages mostly under 30, assessed for re-injury risk. This athlete is an uninjured, 10+-year-trained upper-body lifter, and the question is static tissue girth, not dynamic performance. None of the three — tissue, population, outcome — matches. **Do not import "10% difference = act" from this literature.**
- **ISAK kinanthropometry.** A real, certified measurement discipline with documented technical error of measurement (TEM). The commonly cited standard is **~1% TEM for girths** (vs. 5% for skinfolds) for a certified ("competent" or higher) anthropometrist **[secondary, unverified primary — ISAK standards-manual figures via indexed summaries, not the manual itself]**. Two things limit its relevance here: (a) that 1% is inter/intra-*observer* error for a trained technician using a narrow steel tape with a tension gauge and caliper-identified bony landmarks — a materially more controlled protocol than a self-measuring athlete with a cloth tape and a pen mark; (b) it describes repeatability on the *same* occasion, not week-to-week physiological change. Treat 1% as a best-case floor for a trained third party, not an estimate of this athlete's own error.
- **What "normal" side-to-side difference looks like in uninjured people — the two most load-bearing numbers I could actually verify:**
  - **Strength asymmetry, healthy active adults** (Bishop et al.-type design, read in full via PMC9319678: n = 55, 31 M/24 F, mean age 30, "physically active," cross-sectional, elbow flexor/extensor maximum voluntary force): average dominant-side advantage only **3–6%**, and critically, *direction* was muscle- and metric-specific — 56% of people were actually stronger or symmetric on the non-dominant side for elbow flexors. At the individual level, **>65% of participants showed an asymmetry larger than a smallest-worthwhile-change threshold on at least one metric.** The paper's own conclusion: assess each muscle individually; a single uniform threshold across muscles is not defensible. **Population caveat: general physically-active adults, not resistance-trained lifters specifically, and strength (force output) not circumference. Confidence: moderate that some single-digit-percent asymmetry is the norm, not the exception, in uninjured people; low confidence that this specific 3–6% figure transfers numerically to girth.**
  - **DXA-measured lean-mass asymmetry, trained combat athletes** (read in full via PMC11860482: n = 17 male Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu athletes, mean 7.9 years' experience, competitive): statistically significant right-side predominance, **5.24% total mass, 5.11% fat-free mass, 9.20% fat mass** in the upper limbs. **Population caveat: unilateral grip/throw-dominant combat-sport loading is a different stimulus from a largely bilateral barbell/machine programme, which plausibly inflates this number relative to what a lifter should expect — but it is real girth-adjacent (DXA lean mass) data in trained, healthy people, which the strength-asymmetry study above is not. Confidence: moderate that ~5% is a realistic ceiling for "unremarkable" in a well-trained upper body, low confidence that it's the right number for this specific athlete's more bilateral training.**
  - **Bridging these to a usable convention (this step is inference, not evidence):** a low single-digit-to-~5% side-to-side difference in upper-limb circumference is a reasonable floor for "within normal variation, do not act" in a trained, uninjured lifter. This is built by analogy across two different metrics (force output; DXA lean mass) and should be labeled as such whenever it's used, not presented as a measured fact for girth specifically.
- **Photogrammetric postural assessment (physiotherapy).** This is a real sub-field with genuine reliability work — software such as SAPO, PostureScreen-type tools, and newer smartphone apps report acceptable-to-high intra/inter-rater reliability for *angular* postural measures (e.g., head/shoulder alignment angles) **[secondary, unverified primary for all of the specific software names and ICC figures — I did not open any of these papers directly, only search summaries]**. Two relevance limits: (1) this literature validates *angles* (how tilted is the pelvis, how forward is the head) against a camera, not *girth* or *muscle size*, so it's the right methodology for a different question (posture, not hypertrophy); (2) it still requires exactly the thing this athlete's capture lacks — a controlled, calibrated camera position and a true vertical reference, which is the actual lesson worth importing (see Finding 3 and the Appendix), not the angle-measurement software itself.

**Bottom line for Q2:** there is no validated protocol for assessing *muscle-size* asymmetry from photographs or tape, in any field searched. LSI and ISAK are real frameworks worth knowing about, but neither one is "the" answer here, and both conventions (90% LSI, 1% ISAK TEM) would be misapplied if imported directly. What correctly generalises from this literature is not a number but a habit: don't trust a single threshold across different muscles/metrics, and don't claim precision your measurement protocol can't support.

### 3. What does the tape protocol have to be to be worth trusting?

**Finding: the landmarks in `coach/PHYSIQUE.md` are reasonable; the missing piece is a stated, defensible noise floor, and the honest answer is that no single week will clear it.**

- **Landmarks.** Relaxed-arm girth at the acromion–olecranon midpoint, and flexed-arm girth at maximum girth with elbow at 90°, are standard convention consistent with ISAK-style site definitions. Calf at standing maximum girth, weight even, is likewise standard. **No change recommended here.**
- **Reliability data closest to this actual use case** (read in full via rps.researchcommons.org, vol. 33 iss. 2: n = 46 college students, 6 M/40 F, mean age ~20, no neuromuscular/skeletal disorders, four limb sites, tested one week apart): **intra-rater ICC ≥ 0.92, inter-rater ICC ≥ 0.95 for upper-limb sites** (lower-limb inter-rater only 0.74). The paper's own minimal-detectable-change figures: **>0.6 cm for upper-limb circumference, >1.3 cm for lower-limb circumference** are needed before a change is distinguishable from measurement error. **Population and protocol caveats, stated plainly: testers were examiners following a standardized protocol, not self-measurers; subjects were young, mostly female, non-athletic college students, not a muscular adult male with a larger absolute girth.** Both caveats point the same direction — self-measurement with a home cloth tape probably has *more* error than this, not less, so treat 0.6 cm as a floor, not a target.
- **Self-measurement can be nearly as good as technician measurement when instructions are clear** — self-vs-technician ICCs of 0.97 (waist), 0.96 (hip), 0.84 (neck) have been reported **[secondary, unverified primary]** — but these are torso/neck sites, which `coach/PHYSIQUE.md` itself already correctly excludes as posture/breathing-confounded. I found no equivalent self-vs-technician validity number for arm or calf specifically.
- **Why a weekly comparison is close to meaningless for this athlete specifically:** the BRIEF already characterises him as close to his natural limit, where "progress is slow." Even in a considerably less advanced population, reported hypertrophy rates translate to roughly half a percent of circumference per week or less once cross-sectional-area growth is converted to a linear (circumference) scale **[low confidence, illustrative only — derived from a single, unverified secondary description of an 8-week preacher-curl CSA study, not a direct circumference measurement, and not from a population matched to this athlete's training age]**. On a ~35–40 cm arm, half a percent per week is roughly 1–2 mm — well inside the ~6–13 mm noise band established above. **The direct consequence: do not review the tape week-to-week. Take it weekly (cheap, builds a trend line), but only interpret change at the same cadence as a training block** (the programme already runs 5-week mesocycles — reviewing tape on that boundary is the natural fit, not inventing a new cadence).
- **The pump is a real, separate confound on top of measurement error**, not instead of it: a transient circumference increase on the order of ~1.5 cm (reported for shoulder girth, 15–30 min post-exercise, resolving within about 30 minutes) has been reported **[secondary, unverified primary]**. This is why "before training, not after" in `coach/PHYSIQUE.md` is correct and should stay, and why measurement should also avoid the 24–72h window after directly training the muscle being measured (muscle-damage-related fluid shift — mechanistically well established, not cited to a specific girth-magnitude paper here).
- **For the asymmetry question specifically, same-day repeat spread beats any borrowed literature threshold.** Left and right, measured in the same sitting with the same tape by the same person, share every source of error except the landmark-finding and tension on that one occasion — they don't share week-to-week biological drift or a different tape/technique. So: **take three reps per side, same sitting; if the gap between the two sides' medians is no larger than the spread the athlete sees *within* one side across his own three reps, there is no finding.** This is a direct extension of the principle `coach/PHYSIQUE.md` already states ("that spread is your measurement error") to the symmetry question, and it's a better instrument than importing 0.6 cm/1% TEM/10% LSI from someone else's protocol, because it's calibrated to his actual hands, his actual tape, every time.
- **Concrete recommended protocol change:** always three reps per site per side, full tape removal between each; record all three; track the median; treat <~1 cm weekly change in arm circumference (relaxed or flexed) and <~1.3–1.5 cm in calf as noise **as a starting convention**, to be replaced by the athlete's own within-side repeat spread once a few sessions of data exist; review trend at block boundaries, not weekly; keep capture ≥48h from training the muscle in question; keep the existing before-training, consistent-hydration instruction.

## Recommendations

These are addressed to the specialist named, per the brief — none of them changes the programme on their own.

1. **To `hypertrophy-coach`:** do not treat a visual "lagging" impression from these frames as a basis for adding volume to any muscle. The programme's existing fractional-set bands (SYNTHESIS §2.1) already encode priority; a photo cannot add a validated third signal on top of them, and nor can a report built on too little data. A genuine volume-band shortfall is a log fact, not a photo fact, but it needs a usable log first.
2. **To `hypertrophy-coach` and the athlete jointly:** adopt the corrected tape protocol above (three reps, median, ≥48h from training, block-cadence review) before any tape number is used for a decision. No tape data exists yet, so this is pure protocol-setting, actionable immediately.
3. **To the athlete (capture, not a specialist decision):** fix the capture problems described in the dated capture audit (`coach/reports/`) before the next session — a body-plane reference and full-body framing first, camera registration second (see that audit for why that order, reversed from my own first draft).
4. **To `program-reviewer`:** flag any future recommendation that cites a specific asymmetry percentage (e.g., "left arm is 8% smaller") as requiring the athlete's own repeat-measurement spread as a denominator, not a literature constant — per Finding 3, the literature constants here are weakly sourced or drawn from the wrong population. Also flag any recommendation drawn from a review report whose period is too short, whose adherence is incomplete, or whose sets lack RPE.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions

- **The "only 24% of uninjured athletes pass 90% LSI" claim, if verified, would be the strongest single argument against ever importing a round-number symmetry threshold into this project** — but I could not verify it, and it should not be relied upon until someone traces it to Grindem, Schmitt, or an equivalent primary source.
- **Whether ~3–7% is really the right "don't act" floor for girth specifically is an inference bridging strength and DXA lean-mass data, not a girth measurement itself.** If a future search turns up actual circumference-asymmetry data in trained lifters (not combat athletes, not strength-only), that should replace this estimate.
- **The growth-rate figure used to argue against weekly review (≈0.5%/week circumference) is weakly sourced (single secondary description, untraced population) and illustrative only.** The argument does not depend on this number being exactly right — the measurement-error numbers alone (6–13 mm) are large relative to any plausible single-week change in an advanced lifter — but if the hypertrophy-coach's own research has a better-sourced rate for trained/advanced populations, use that instead.
- **If the athlete's actual home-measurement technique turns out to have much tighter repeat spread than the literature conventions assume** (plausible, since he's measuring the same two sites every week and will get practised at it), the ~1 cm/1.3–1.5 cm noise floors recommended above should shrink accordingly — they are explicitly a starting point, not a ceiling on precision.
