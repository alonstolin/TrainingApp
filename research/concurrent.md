# Concurrent Training — Research Findings

Scope: how lifting and running interact for this athlete — interference size, training-status
scaling, upper vs lower body, dose, modality, sequencing, cross-modal recovery, periodising the
two together, energy availability, and a direct critique of the weekly template. Not covered
here (other specialists): strength progression internals, hypertrophy volume, the run ladder
itself, core. Where a conclusion leans on one of those, the dependency is stated.

Evidence-tier labels follow the brief: T1 meta-analysis/SR · T2 RCT in trained · T3 RCT in
untrained · T4 acute/mechanistic · T5 expert opinion.

## Summary

- **The interference effect on maximal strength and whole-muscle hypertrophy is, on the pooled evidence, close to zero** (Schumann 2022: strength SMD −0.06, hypertrophy −0.01; 43 studies, n = 1,090). What is reliably impaired is *explosive* strength/power (SMD −0.28 to −0.35). This athlete has no power goal.
- **What interference there is lives in the lower body.** Huiberts 2024 (59 studies, n = 1,346): lower-body strength in males SMD −0.43 (−0.64 to −0.22); upper-body strength shows no detectable interference in any subgroup (13 studies). Acute data agree: prior aerobic work cuts leg-press reps 25 % at 4 h but leaves bench press untouched (Sporer & Wenger 2003). His three goal lifts and all four priority muscles are upper-body.
- **There is no evidence at all on athletes like him.** Across 59 studies Huiberts found *zero* with "highly trained" strength status (a cut-off of only bench > 100 % BW, squat > 120 % BW, > 12 months). Petré's "trained" subgroup (SMD −0.35) was ≥ 3 months of structured training — rugby/soccer/basketball players with "minimal experience in resistance training", 14/15 arms running, driven by one study. Nothing in the literature describes a 10-year lifter.
- **Wilson 2012's "running interferes, cycling doesn't" has not replicated for strength or whole-muscle size.** Schumann 2022 and Petré 2021 found no modality moderator; a 2023 network meta-analysis found moderate-intensity *cycling* + RT the worst arm for lower-limb strength (SMD −0.38) with moderate running intermediate (−0.25). Only type I *fibre* CSA still shows a running signal (Lundberg 2022: −0.81, from three running studies).
- **Dose: 2–3 easy runs of 30–70 min/week is at or below the doses that produce the null pooled effects** (Petré: 2.6–2.9 sessions/wk, 29–37 min mean). Wilson's negative correlations (frequency r −0.26 to −0.35; duration r −0.29 to −0.75) came from ranges reaching 4–6 sessions/wk and 60+ min. He will brush the low edge only in the last four weeks of the ladder.
- **Separation matters; order barely does.** Interference shows up in same-session designs (Petré trained: −0.66 same session vs −0.10 separated > 2 h; Schumann explosive: −0.31 same session vs NS at ≥ 3 h; Robineau: 0 h impaired, 6 h and 24 h matched strength-only; Sale 1990: alternate days +25 % vs same day +13 % leg press). Every run in the program is on a non-lifting day. That is right and should not change.
- **The one placement the acute evidence argues against is the Tuesday easy run 24 h after Monday's lower session.** Doma & Deakin 2013 (14 trained runners): running economy 5–10 % worse and time-to-exhaustion ~30 % shorter the day after a lower-body strength session; Doma 2017 review: impairment lasts "several hours to days". For a novice runner on non-runner tissues, that is the worst 24 h of the week to run in.
- **The heavy Sunday OHP the morning after the long run is, on the acute evidence, probably fine**: effects of prior aerobic work are localised to the muscles used and clear within 8–24 h (Sporer & Wenger; Leveritt 2000 in long-history lifters). My earlier concern about that slot is downgraded to low confidence.
- **The two periodisation clocks (5-week lifting block, 4-week run cycle) are unsynchronised, and on the most likely offset the 10K lands on the RPE-9 lifting week.** The evidence supports aligning recovery weeks (deload weeks are not free — Coleman 2024) and treating only *legs*, not upper body, as a maintenance phase in the final run weeks (Spiering 2021; Doma 2013).
- **Energy availability is the one mechanism by which running plausibly costs him muscle**: RT in energy deficit loses lean-mass gains (ES −0.57) but not strength (Murphy & Koehler 2022), cliff ≈ −500 kcal/day. Two to three runs add ~900–1,800 kcal/week at his mass; unmatched, that is a chronic small deficit at his ceiling.
- **Biggest change suggested:** get the easy run out of the 24-h window after legs. Cleanest version: rotate the lifting cycle one slot so Lower falls on Sunday, the day after the long run, consolidating all leg stress into Sat–Sun and putting both runs ≥ 48 h from any leg session. Minimal version: move the easy run from Tuesday to Thursday. Confidence: moderate that the current slot is wrong; moderate-low that the rotation is the best fix.

## Where the current program is wrong, unsupported, or suboptimal

1. **"Wilson 2012 (interference)" as a design premise.** The program treats interference as a live threat to be managed. Wilson's 2012 effect sizes (CT vs ST: hypertrophy 0.85 vs 1.23; strength 1.44 vs 1.76) have not survived: Schumann 2022's update on 43 studies puts the difference at −0.01 and −0.06, and Huiberts 2024 finds upper-body strength untouched. Wilson's modality finding (running worse than cycling) did not replicate as a moderator in Schumann 2022, Petré 2021 or Lundberg's whole-muscle data. **High confidence** that the program over-weights interference on hypertrophy and max strength for a lifter whose priorities are all upper-body.

2. **"Petré 2021 / Schumann 2022 (separate-day concurrent training)" — right conclusion, mis-described population.** Petré's trained-subgroup interference (−0.35) comes from team-sport athletes with ≥ 3 months of structured training and "minimal experience in resistance training", mostly running intervals, mostly same-session, driven by Robineau's two arms; it does not describe an advanced lifter. The separate-day recommendation stands anyway (Petré −0.10 separated; Robineau 24 h = strength-only; Sale 1990 alternate-day 25 % vs 13 %), but the reason is *fatigue*, not molecular interference. **High confidence** on the mis-description; **moderate-high** that the conclusion survives.

3. **Tuesday easy run the day after Monday lower.** The program's rule — "no two consecutive days share a primary muscle" — is applied to lifting days and silently waived for the run. Doma & Deakin 2013 shows running measurably impaired 24 h after lower-body RT in trained runners; Doma 2017 puts residual impairment at hours to days. The cost for a novice runner is not race pace, it is run quality and (my inference) injury exposure on fatigued, less-coordinated legs. **Moderate confidence** this is the weakest slot in the week; Thursday or a Sunday leg day are both cleaner.

4. **The 12-week RCT (24 h vs 48–72 h, no difference) is being stretched across modalities and populations.** It is Yang et al. 2018 (*Front Physiol*): 30 recreationally active, resistance-*untrained* men (leg press 70 → 126 kg over the study), 3 × 10 at 10RM, lifting only, no running. Whatever it shows about spacing resistance sessions in novices, it is not evidence about running the day after a leg session — the outcome it measured was lifting and the fatigue Doma's work shows carrying over (running economy, MVC, soreness) was never tested. **High confidence** the citation does not reach this use.

5. **Legs and the long run are five days apart; the easy run and legs are one day apart.** The template protects the gap that needs least protection (Robineau: 24 h sufficed for strength outcomes) and leaves open the one the acute data flag. **Moderate confidence.**

6. **The 5-week lifting mesocycle and the 14-week run ladder are on different clocks.** With lifting week 1 ≈ run week 6 (the brief's "around week 5–6"), lifting week 4 (RPE 9, +2 accessory sets) lands on run week 9 and again on run **week 14 — the 10K itself** — while the lifting deloads fall on run weeks 10 and 15, neither of which is a running down-week. Nothing in the program couples them. Deload weeks carry a small strength cost of their own (Coleman 2024), so paying for them twice, out of phase, is the worst arrangement. **High confidence** on the arithmetic; **moderate** on the fix (Q8).

7. **No energy-intake provision.** The program adds a rising running load to a lifter at his muscular ceiling with no mention of intake, and the one null RCT in resistance-trained men (Dolan 2024) explicitly conditions its result on positive energy balance. Murphy & Koehler 2022 makes energy deficit the best-supported route by which cardio "costs gains". **Moderate-high confidence** this deserves a line in the program.

## Findings

### Q1. Interference effect — current evidence

**Finding.** Pooled across everything (mostly untrained/recreationally active subjects), adding endurance training to resistance training does not measurably reduce maximal strength or whole-muscle hypertrophy. It reduces explosive strength/power by a small-to-moderate amount, most clearly when both are done in the same session. The picture has changed materially since 2012: Wilson's moderate interference on hypertrophy and strength has shrunk to null in the larger, better-controlled 2021–2024 syntheses. Fibre-level (biopsy) data still show a small type I signal that whole-muscle imaging does not; the field has not reconciled the two.

- **Wilson et al. 2012**, *J Strength Cond Res* 26(8):2293 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/). Meta-analysis, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes. Population: mixed, predominantly untrained/recreationally active; not subgrouped by status. ES for strength-only / endurance-only / concurrent: hypertrophy 1.23 / 0.27 / 0.85; strength 1.76 / 0.78 / 1.44; power 0.91 / 0.11 / 0.55. Running (not cycling) with RT: significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Negative correlations with endurance frequency (r −0.26 to −0.35) and duration (r −0.29 to −0.75). *T1 · confidence that these numbers are now superseded: high.* Abstract only — full text paywalled.
- **Schumann, Feuerbacher, Sünkeler, Freitag, Rønnestad, Doma, Lundberg 2022**, *Sports Med* 52:601 — [PMC8891239](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/). Meta-analysis, 43 studies, n = 1,090 (590 CT, 500 ST). Population: "untrained" or "active" only; no advanced lifters. Hypertrophy SMD −0.01 (95 % CI −0.16 to 0.18, p = 0.919; 15 studies, n = 389). Maximal strength −0.06 (−0.20 to 0.09, p = 0.446; 37 studies, n = 967). Explosive strength −0.28 (−0.48 to −0.08, p = 0.007; 18 studies, n = 478); same-session −0.31 (−0.62 to −0.01, p = 0.043); separated ≥ 3 h NS. No significant moderation by modality, frequency, training status or age. No upper/lower split. Mean PEDro 4.3 ± 0.9. *T1 · high for the pooled null; population caveat applies.*
- **Lundberg, Feuerbacher, Sünkeler, Schumann 2022**, *Sports Med* 52:2391 — [PMC9474354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474354/). Meta-analysis of fibre-CSA outcomes, 15 RCTs, n = 300, mostly untrained/active. Fibre CSA SMD −0.23 (−0.46 to −0.00, p = 0.050); type I −0.34 (−0.72 to 0.04); type II −0.13 (−0.39 to 0.12). Running subgroup, type I: −0.81 (−1.26 to −0.36) from **3 running studies vs 12 cycling**. Whole-muscle (MRI/CT) hypertrophy not compromised. *T1 · moderate on the small fibre signal; low on the running subgroup.*
- **Huiberts, Wüst, van der Zwaard 2024**, *Sports Med* 54:485 — [open PDF](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/141008429/Concurrent-strength-and-endurance-training.pdf). Meta-analysis, 59 studies, n = 1,346. Lower-body strength: males SMD −0.43 (−0.64 to −0.22, p < 0.001, 17 studies, I² = 0 %); females 0.08 (−0.34 to 0.49); combined −0.32. Power combined −0.35 (males −0.27, females −0.52). Upper-body strength: no significant effect (13 studies). Hypertrophy: 4 studies, no conclusion. *T1 · high.* Detail under Q2 and Q3.
- **Chen, Feng, Huang, Mi 2024**, *J Exerc Sci Fit* 22:86 — [PMC10767279](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10767279). Network meta-analysis, 40 studies, n = 841, mixed status (18–45 y). Lower-limb 1RM vs RT alone: HIIT running −0.08 (−0.25 to 0.08); HIIT cycling −0.18 (−0.49 to 0.13); MICT running −0.25 (−0.46 to −0.05); MICT cycling −0.38 (−0.62 to −0.14). Muscle CSA: all near zero, no differences. 67.5 % same-session designs. *T1 · moderate; same-session weighting inflates the strength SMDs relative to separated-day training.*
- **Held, Wolf, Rappelt, Bloch, Donath, Micke, Geisler, Isenmann 2026**, *Sports Med* (umbrella review) — [PubMed 41762427](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41762427/), abstract via Europe PMC. 17 meta-analyses, 144 studies, n = 1,492. Concurrent training develops both qualities in recreationally trained people; no significant sequence effect; "data from highly trained to elite athletes remain scarce". *T1 · moderate (abstract only).*

### Q2. Does it scale with training status?

**Finding.** Nobody has measured it in anyone like him. Two syntheses point opposite ways, and both use definitions of "trained" that he exceeds by an order of magnitude. Petré 2021 found interference only in its "trained" tier (SMD −0.35) — ≥ 3 months of structured training, team-sport athletes with "minimal experience in resistance training", almost all running, mostly same-session, driven by Robineau's two arms. Huiberts 2024, with a stricter status framework, found lower-body interference in *untrained*-strength subjects (p = 0.02) and *not* in trained-strength subjects (p = 0.14), and likewise for power (p = 0.01 vs 0.42). Schumann 2022 found no status moderation. The Coffey & Hawley hypothesis that interference grows near the ceiling remains a hypothesis; the only RCT in genuinely resistance-trained men (Dolan 2024, cycling, n = 23) found nothing. **I am inferring** that for an advanced lifter the molecular interference is not the issue; the fatigue and recovery budget is, because his margin for wasted sessions is small and his per-session recovery demand is high.

- **Petré, Hemmingsson, Rosdahl, Psilander 2021**, *Sports Med* 51:991 — [PMC8053170](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8053170/). Meta-analysis of lower-body 1RM, 27 studies, n = 750 (7 untrained, 10 moderately trained, 10 trained). Untrained SMD 0.03 (−0.29 to 0.35); moderately trained −0.20 (−0.42 to 0.02); trained −0.35 (−0.59 to −0.11, p < 0.01). Trained, same-session (< 20 min apart) −0.66 (−1.08 to −0.25); trained, separated (> 2 h) −0.10 (−0.43 to 0.23). "Trained" = regular structured training ≥ 3 months; contributing studies Balabinis, Cantrell, Chtara, Dolezal & Potteiger, Hennessy & Watson, Kraemer, Mirghani, Panissa, Robineau ×2 — team-sport or general athletes, no advanced lifters. 14/15 trained-arm interventions used running. Endurance dose similar across tiers (2.6–2.9 sessions/wk, 29–37 min). Authors: "no highly trained category… insufficient studies"; trained tier "driven by two interventions from the same study". *T1 · high in the numbers; low that they transfer to this athlete.*
- **Huiberts 2024** (above). Strength-status classification (Table 2): untrained < 60 % BW bench / < 80 % BW squat; trained 60–100 % / 80–120 %, 2–12 months; highly trained > 100 % / > 120 %, > 12 months. Of 38 studies with classifiable strength status: **27 untrained, 11 trained, 0 highly trained.** Lower-body strength blunted in untrained-strength (p = 0.02), NS in trained-strength (p = 0.14); power impaired in untrained (p = 0.01), not trained (p = 0.42); upper-body strength unaffected in both. *T1 · high that the literature contains no one at his level.*
- **Schumann 2022** (above): status (untrained vs active) not a significant moderator for any outcome. *T1.*
- **Dolan, Quiles, Goldsmith et al. 2024**, *J Hum Kinet* 91:87 — [PMC11057620](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057620/). RCT, 8 wk, n = 23 resistance-trained men (≥ 2 y, squat ≥ 1.5 × BW, bench ≥ 1.25 × BW), 3 × RT/wk + 2 × 30 min cycling on non-RT days (HIIT or moderate) or barbell circuit or RT only. Squat gains: RT 20.4 kg, moderate-cycling 25.5, HIIT-cycling 16.9; bench ~10 kg all groups; quadriceps and pectoralis thickness no meaningful contrasts. All in positive energy balance. Authors: chief limitation is n. *T2 (the only RCT in resistance-trained subjects found) · low-moderate: cycling not running, ~6 per group, 8 weeks.*
- **Leveritt, MacLaughlin, Abernethy 2000**, *J Sports Sci* 18:865 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11144863/). Acute crossover, n = 8 men "with a long history of resistance training"; 50 min cycling at 70–110 % critical power; isokinetic, isometric and isotonic leg-extension strength unchanged at 8 h and 32 h vs control (p > 0.05). *T4 in trained lifters · moderate: the one acute datum in long-history lifters shows fast recovery from endurance work.*
- **Coffey & Hawley 2017**, *J Physiol* 595:2883, "Concurrent exercise training: do opposites distract?" — hypothesis that interference grows with training history; cited by Huiberts as unconfirmed. *T4/T5.*

### Q3. What is affected — lower vs upper body

**Finding.** Interference, where it exists, is a lower-body phenomenon. Upper-body strength shows no detectable interference in the largest synthesis that separated it (Huiberts, 13 studies), in either sex or any status tier. Acutely, prior aerobic work reduces leg-press volume by 25 % at 4 h and 9 % at 8 h while bench press is unaffected — the impairment is "localized to the muscle groups involved in the aerobic training" (Sporer & Wenger). Direct upper-body hypertrophy data under running are thin, but the one trained-lifter RCT (cycling) showed pectoralis growth unaffected, and Robineau found bench gains unaffected at 6 h and 24 h separation and impaired only with zero gap. The mechanistic case is one-sided: running loads the legs eccentrically and depletes leg glycogen; delts, arms and lats do neither. His hypertrophy priority is therefore largely insulated. The residual risks to upper-body work are systemic — sleep, energy balance, and same-day proximity (Ratamess: even easy running 10 min before lifting cuts reps on every lift) — not local interference.

- **Huiberts 2024** (above): upper-body strength SMD not significant for males or females (p = 0.67 for sex difference), point estimates near zero across untrained/trained strength- and endurance-status tiers (Fig. 4–5). *T1 · high for strength; hypertrophy not separable.*
- **Sporer & Wenger 2003**, *J Strength Cond Res* 17(4):638 — [journal](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/abstract/2003/11000/effects_of_aerobic_exercise_on_strength.3.aspx), abstract via Europe PMC. n = 16 men, 4 sets bench and leg press at ~75 % 1RM at 4 h, 8 h, 24 h after interval or continuous cycling, plus control. Leg press reps reduced at 4 h (−25 %) and 8 h (−9 %), normal at 24 h; bench press unaffected at every point; no difference between aerobic intensities. *T4 · moderate (cycling; status unspecified).*
- **Robineau, Babault, Piscione, Lacome, Bigard 2016**, *J Strength Cond Res* 30(3):672 — [open PDF](https://formation.ffr.fr/sites/default/files/documents/doc/2016-12/Specific%20training%20effects%20of%20concurrent%20aerobic%20and%20strength%20exercises%20depend%20on%20recovery%20duration.pdf). RCT, 7 wk, n = 58 amateur rugby players with "only minimal experience in resistance training"; strength always first, then 3 × 6 min of 15 s/15 s running at 120 % MAV, at 0 h / 6 h / 24 h; 2 × each per week. Bench 1RM: C-0h +8.3 %, C-6h +14.1 %, C-24h +11.4 %, STR +11.6 %; half-squat +16.8 % / +31.2 % / +25.9 % / +23.9 %. *T3 · moderate that upper-body strength is at risk only with zero separation.*
- **Dolan 2024** (above): pectoralis thickness not reduced in either cycling group. *T2 · low-moderate.*
- **Ratamess et al. 2016**, *J Strength Cond Res* 30(10):2667 — [journal](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/nsca-jscr/fulltext/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001548~acute-resistance-exercise-performance-is-negatively-impacted), abstract via Europe PMC. n = 11 resistance-trained men; treadmill running (45 min at 60 % VO₂R; 20 min at 75 %; intervals; uphill) 10 min before squat, deadlift, high pull, bench, push press at 70–80 % 1RM. 9.1–18.6 % fewer total reps vs control; squat most affected; velocity/power down for bench too. *T4 · moderate: shows same-day proximity hurts all lifts, which is exactly what the template avoids.*
- **Kraemer et al. 1995**, *J Appl Physiol* 78:976 — [journal](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/jappl.1995.78.3.976), abstract via Europe PMC. 12 wk, n = 35 men, groups C (whole-body strength + endurance), UC (upper-body strength + endurance), E, ST; treadmill. C and ST increased 1RM on all lifts; type I fibre area rose in ST, fell in E; IIa rose in ST and C. Secondary sources describe upper-body "compartmentalisation"; the abstract does not use the word and I could not open the full text, so I cite it only as consistent. *T3 · low-moderate.*

### Q4. Dose

**Finding.** Two to three conversational runs a week, growing from ~35 min to ~65–70 min for the long run, sits within the dose range across which the pooled effect on strength and hypertrophy is null. Petré's tiers averaged 2.6–2.9 sessions/wk at 29–37 min and showed no interference when separated from lifting. Wilson's dose correlations are real but were generated across a range reaching 4–6 sessions/wk and 60+ min, with the steepest slope (duration, r to −0.75) for power. No study identifies a sharp threshold; the field's practical reading (Schumann; Stronger by Science synthesis) is that interference becomes plausible when *total* load exceeds what recovery, sleep and intake cover — reached sooner by lifters already at high resistance volumes. That is him: the running dose is small, but so is his slack.

- **Wilson 2012** (above): frequency r −0.26 to −0.35; duration r −0.29 to −0.75. *T1 · moderate on direction; low on any threshold.*
- **Petré 2021** (above): 2.6–2.9 sessions/wk, 29–37 min; "frequency and duration could not explain between-group differences". *T1.*
- **Schumann 2022** (above): frequency not a significant moderator. *T1.*
- **Dolan 2024** (above): 2 × 30 min/wk on non-lifting days, no interference in resistance-trained men (cycling). *T2.*
- **Stronger by Science research spotlight** on Schumann 2022 — [link](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-interference-effect/). Expert synthesis: caution mainly for compromised recovery, those already at high endurance or resistance loads, and power athletes. *T5.*

Dependency: the exact weekly minutes at the top of the ladder (5 km + 10 km ≈ 33 + 67 min at ~6:40/km, plus an optional third run) are the running specialist's domain; the interference conclusion holds for anything ≤ ~3 h/week easy.

### Q5. Modality — running vs cycling

**Finding.** Wilson's modality finding is not robust at the whole-muscle or strength level. Three later syntheses with more studies found no running-vs-cycling moderation (Schumann 2022; Petré 2021 — 14/15 of whose "trained" arms ran, so a running penalty would have surfaced there), and the 2024 network meta-analysis ranked moderate cycling *worse* than moderate running for lower-limb strength. The one place running still shows a signal is type I fibre CSA (Lundberg: −0.81 from three studies). Mechanistically the eccentric load and muscle damage of running is real — and is why running impairs *next-day leg performance* more than cycling would — but for a lifter who must run, that is a scheduling problem, not a reason to expect worse upper-body outcomes. He should not treat running as more dangerous to his goals than cycling; he should treat it as more fatiguing to the legs and schedule accordingly.

- **Wilson 2012**: running, not cycling, decremented hypertrophy and strength. *T1 (2012 data).*
- **Schumann 2022**: modality not a significant moderator for any outcome. *T1 · high.*
- **Lundberg 2022**: running type I −0.81 (−1.26 to −0.36), 3 studies; cycling NS. *T1 · low.*
- **Chen 2024 NMA**: lower-limb 1RM MICT-cycling −0.38 vs MICT-running −0.25 vs HIIT-running −0.08; CSA no differences. *T1 · moderate.*
- **Petré 2021**: no modality difference; trained tier almost all running. *T1.*

### Q6. Sequencing

**Finding.** The evidence supports (a) separating lifting and running into different sessions, ideally different days; (b) if same-day, ≥ 6 h apart, lifting first when lower-body strength is the priority; (c) that order matters far less than separation. Same-session designs are where most historical interference comes from. On the template: putting every run on a non-lifting day is correct and evidence-backed. The five-day gap between legs and the long run is more than any study required (24 h sufficed in Robineau; 24 h also restored leg-press volume in Sporer & Wenger). The 24-h gap between legs and the easy run is the one the acute literature says is too short for a quality run.

- **Petré 2021**: trained tier, same session (< 20 min) −0.66 vs separated (> 2 h) −0.10. *T1 · moderate (population caveat).*
- **Schumann 2022**: explosive strength same-session −0.31, separated ≥ 3 h NS; hypertrophy and max strength null either way. *T1 · high.*
- **Robineau 2016** (above): 0 h < 6 h ≈ 24 h ≈ strength-only for bench and half-squat; MVC at 180°/s higher in 24 h and STR than 0 h/6 h; VO₂peak best in 24 h. *T3 · moderate.*
- **Sale, Jacobs, MacDougall, Garner 1990**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 22(3):348 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2381303/), abstract via Europe PMC. n = 15 young men, 20 wk; same-day (S + E together, 2 d/wk, n = 7) vs alternate-day (S 2 d, E 2 other d, n = 8); S = 6–8 sets 15–20RM leg press; E = 6–8 × 3 min cycling at 90–100 % VO₂max. Leg press 1RM +25 % alternate vs +13 % same-day (p < 0.05); knee-extensor CSA +34 % vs +31 %, fibre area +25 % vs +33 %, VO₂max +6 % vs +7 % — all NS. *T3 · moderate: separation helped strength, not size.*
- **Eddens, van Someren, Howatson 2018**, *Sports Med* 48:177 — [PMC5752732](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5752732/). Meta-analysis of intra-session order: RT→ET gave ~7 % larger 1RM squat gains than ET→RT; no order effect on hypertrophy, static strength or CRF. *T1 · moderate.*
- **Held 2026 umbrella**: no significant sequence effect across 17 meta-analyses. *T1 · moderate (abstract).*
- **Doma & Deakin 2013**, *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 38:651 — [journal](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/apnm-2012-0362), abstract via Europe PMC. Crossover, n = 14 trained/moderately trained male runners; strength and running 6 h apart in either order; running economy next day. Cost of running at 70 % VT 0.72 → 0.76, at 90 % VT 0.70 → 0.77 (strength-first); time to exhaustion 335 s → 238 s (strength-first) / 275 s (run-first). *T4 · moderate.*

### Q7. Recovery across modalities

**Finding.**

*Heavy leg day → run 24 h later:* measurably impaired. Doma & Deakin 2013: running economy 5–10 % worse and time-to-exhaustion ~30 % shorter the day after lower-body strength work in trained runners. Doma 2012 (n = 12): same-day strength + endurance raised next-day cost of running and RPE and lowered MVC. Doma, Deakin & Bentley 2017 (review): impairment persists "several hours to days", via reduced neural drive, worse movement economy, soreness and glycogen depletion. 48–72 h has not been directly tested; the acute data stop showing effects somewhere past 24 h. An advanced lifter's repeated-bout protection likely shrinks, but does not remove, this.

*Long run → upper-body lifting 24 h later:* no evidence of impairment. Sporer & Wenger: bench press unaffected even 4 h after cycling while leg press was down 25 %; Leveritt 2000: leg strength itself unaffected at 8 h and 32 h in long-history lifters after 50 min hard cycling; Robineau: bench gains at 24 h separation identical to strength-only. The caveat is that all of this is cycling or short intervals — a 60–70 min run adds eccentric damage cycling lacks — but that damage is in the legs. The systemic residue (sleep, glycogen, appetite) is real but small for an easy run and, on the evidence, does not show up in upper-body performance. **My earlier concern about heavy OHP the morning after the long run is downgraded to low confidence.**

*Long run → leg session 24 h later:* not directly tested with running. Leveritt (cycling) says no impairment at 32 h in trained lifters; running's eccentric component makes some impairment plausible. For a *maintenance* leg session this is tolerable; for a priority leg session it would not be.

*What should sit next to what:* runs next to upper-body days (either order); legs either consolidated next to the long run (if legs are maintenance) or ≥ 48 h from any run in both directions (if legs matter). Never a run the day after legs when a cleaner slot exists.

### Q8. Periodising the two together

**Finding.** No trial tests deload alignment across modalities; the answer is assembled from four pieces of evidence and one arithmetic fact.

1. *Deload weeks are not free.* Coleman et al. 2024 (PeerJ; resistance-trained; 9 wk; one deload week at the midpoint vs none): the deload group gained less lower-body strength, with no difference in hypertrophy, power or endurance. So each deload should buy something; paying for lifting deloads and running down-weeks in different weeks means paying twice.
2. *Fatigue is systemic, not modality-siloed.* Wilson's dose correlations and Doma's carry-over data show that the running load lands on the same recovery budget as lifting. A running down-week during an RPE-9 lifting week is not a recovery week, and vice versa.
3. *Strength is maintained on very little, provided intensity is kept.* Spiering, Mujika, Sharp, Foulis 2021 (JSCR, narrative review): strength and muscle size maintained up to 32 weeks on as little as one session/week and one set per exercise if intensity is held; endurance maintained on 2 sessions/week or 33–66 % less volume, again if intensity is kept. Rønnestad's in-season cyclist work (~1 heavy session per 10 days maintains strength over 13–15 weeks) agrees. So a lifting "maintenance" week costs almost nothing for a week or two.
4. *Endurance tapers keep intensity and frequency and cut volume 41–60 % for ~2 weeks* (Bosquet 2007 meta-analysis; for a 10K in a novice, 5–7 days is the practitioner norm — the running specialist's call). Upper-body lifting does not impair running (Sporer localisation; Doma's impairments all came from lower-body RT), so there is no evidence-based reason to suspend upper-body lifting in the final weeks. Lower-body lifting does impair next-day running, so *legs* are the thing to put on maintenance and to keep ≥ 48 h from the long run in the last two weeks.

*Should the clocks be coupled?* Yes. The arithmetic: with lifting week 1 ≈ run week 6, the RPE-9 lifting week (wk 4) lands on run weeks 9 and 14 — the second of which is the 10K — and the lifting deloads (run weeks 10 and 15) miss both running down-weeks (8 and 12). **Align, do not alternate:** run the lifting block on the running calendar's 4-week cycle (3 loading + 1 deload) for the rest of the build, so lifting deloads fall on run weeks 8 and 12, and make run week 14 a lifting maintenance week (intensity kept, sets halved, no leg session inside 48 h of the 10K). Then return to 5-week blocks. Alternating (lifting hard while running is easy, and vice versa) is an RP/Israetel-style option for athletes with more days and more slack; for a 4-day lifter with two runs it doubles the number of "hard" weeks and gives no full-recovery week. *Confidence: moderate.* Cost of the change: one 5-week block becomes two 4-week blocks — one fewer loading week over the build — and the lifting deload in run week 14 costs at most one week of upper-body progression (Coleman).

### Q9. Energy availability

**Finding.** The best-supported route by which added running costs a lifter muscle is an unmatched energy deficit, not molecular interference. Murphy & Koehler's meta-regression of RT in energy deficit: lean-mass gains impaired (ES −0.57, p = 0.02), strength not (−0.31, p = 0.28), with the practical cliff at deficits > ~500 kcal/day. Dolan 2024 — the one null RCT in resistance-trained men — explicitly conditions its result on positive energy balance. At ~90+ kg, easy running costs roughly 1 kcal/kg/km (my estimate, standard approximation), so 10–15 km/week now and 15–20 km/week at the top of the ladder is ~900–1,800 kcal/week; unmatched, that is a chronic 130–260 kcal/day deficit — below the cliff, but not nothing for someone at his ceiling with slow gains to lose. The flag: if body mass drifts down during the build, intake is the first lever, not the running. Nutrition specifics are out of scope.

- **Murphy & Koehler 2022**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 32:125 — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sms.14075). Meta-analysis + meta-regression, RCTs of RT in energy deficit ≥ 3 wk. Lean mass ES −0.57 (p = 0.02); strength −0.31 (p = 0.28); avoid deficits > 500 kcal/day. *T1 · moderate: mixed populations; effect is of imposed deficits, not of added cardio per se.* Abstract via search; primary not opened.
- **Dolan 2024** (above): "findings cannot generalise to negative energy balance". *T2.*

### Q10. Critique of the weekly template

Mon lower · Tue easy run + core · Wed push · Thu optional · Fri pull + core · Sat long run + core · Sun delts/arms (OHP heavy).

**What it gets right and should keep.** Every run is on a non-lifting day — the single best-supported concurrent-training decision available (Q6; Ratamess shows what same-day proximity costs). The lifting rotation and the 48-h spacing of the upper sessions are untouched by this critique. The long run is far from the leg session.

**Where the evidence would arrange it differently.**

1. *The Tuesday run is 24 h after the leg session.* The one placement the acute evidence directly argues against (Q7). Moving it does not require touching the lifting days.
2. *Leg stress is spread across the week (Mon legs, Tue run, Sat run)* rather than consolidated, so the runs, not the priority lifts, take the fatigue. Because legs are maintenance and the runs are a goal, that is backwards.
3. *Heavy OHP the morning after the long run* — no evidence against it (Q7); I list it only because it is the most systemically loaded slot as the long run reaches 60–70 min. Low confidence; do not change the template for this alone.

**Proposed rearrangement (primary).** Rotate the four-day lifting cycle by one slot so it starts on Sunday:

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Sat | Long run + core |
| Sun | Lower + pull-up volume + curls |
| Mon | Upper Push — incline bench heavy |
| Tue | Easy run + core |
| Wed | Upper Pull — weighted pull-up heavy (+ core) |
| Thu | Optional: second easy run, or bonus delt/arm day, or rest |
| Fri | Shoulders & Arms — OHP heavy |

The order Lower → Push → Pull → S&A is unchanged, so the program's "cycle regardless of calendar" logic and its no-consecutive-primary-muscle rule both survive (Sun curls / Mon triceps; Mon push / Wed pull; Wed rear delts / Fri face pull is the same light overlap the program already accepts). What changes: all leg lifting and the long run sit in one Sat–Sun block; the easy run is 48 h after legs; the optional third run is 96 h after legs; the long run is six days after legs and follows an upper session rather than preceding one. Cost: the leg session is done 24 h after the long run and will be somewhat weaker — acceptable because legs are explicitly maintenance and Spiering shows maintenance needs little. **Confidence: moderate-low** — an inference from the separation evidence, the acute carry-over data and the athlete's goal ranking; no trial has tested weekly templates in an advanced lifter.

**Conservative alternative** if the lifting days must stay exactly as they are: move the easy run from Tuesday to Thursday (72 h after legs, 48 h before the long run) and make Tuesday the optional slot (rest or delt/arm bonus — *not* a run). This captures most of the value with less disruption. **Confidence: moderate.**

**What I would not change.** Do not put any run on a lifting day to free a slot; same-day/same-session interference is the one part of the literature that has held up.

## Recommendations for this athlete

1. **Keep every run on a non-lifting day.** If ever forced onto the same day, lift first and leave ≥ 6 h (Robineau; Eddens); never run immediately before or after lifting (Ratamess).
2. **Move the easy run out of the 24-h window after legs.** Preferred: rotate the lifting cycle so Lower falls on Sunday (Sat long run → Sun Lower → Mon Push → Tue easy run → Wed Pull → Thu optional → Fri S&A). Minimal: easy run Tuesday → Thursday, Tuesday becomes the non-running optional slot.
3. **Treat the leg session as the flexible one.** If the long run leaves him flat, drop the leg session's top-set RPE by 1 or cut a set; never do that to the incline/OHP/pull-up heavy sessions. Legs are maintenance; Mon/Wed/Fri are the priority.
4. **Do not reduce upper-body volume to "make room for" running.** Nothing in the evidence predicts running will blunt delt/arm/lat hypertrophy or upper-body strength. If the delt/arm progression stalls, look at sleep and intake before blaming the running.
5. **Do not add the optional third run in lifting loading weeks 3–4** (RPE 8.5–9, +1/+2 accessory sets) unless recovery is clearly fine; use it in weeks 1–2 and deload weeks.
6. **Couple the two calendars.** For the remainder of the build, run lifting on 4-week blocks (3 loading + 1 deload) timed so deloads land on the ladder's down-weeks (run weeks 8 and 12). Make run week 14 (the 10K) a lifting maintenance week: top sets at RPE 6–7, sets halved, legs one light session ≥ 48 h before the 10K or none. Return to 5-week blocks after the race. (Depends on the running specialist keeping the ladder's down-weeks at 8 and 12.)
7. **In run weeks 13–14, keep upper-body lifting progressing and put only legs on maintenance** (one session, intensity kept, volume cut ≥ 50 %, ≥ 48 h before the long run).
8. **Raise intake in step with running distance**: roughly +100 kcal per easy km added to the week; weigh weekly; a downward drift > 0.5 kg over two weeks during the build is a nutrition signal, not a training one.
9. **Ignore the running-vs-cycling distinction for planning.** Running is not more dangerous to his upper-body goals than cycling; it is more fatiguing to the legs, which recommendation 2 handles.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions

- **Petré vs Huiberts on training status** point opposite ways (interference rises with status / falls with status). Neither includes anyone near his level; both "trained" tiers are populations he left a decade ago. I weight Huiberts higher (larger, stricter classification, later) but the honest position is "unknown at his level".
- **Fibre-level vs whole-muscle hypertrophy** (Lundberg vs Schumann) disagree and the field has not resolved it. For an upper-body hypertrophy goal it is moot — every biopsy is vastus lateralis.
- **Running vs cycling**: Wilson and Lundberg (fibre) say running is worse; Schumann, Petré and Chen say no or the reverse. I side with the larger, later data but note the fibre signal.
- **Long run → next-day leg session** has no running-specific data; Leveritt's null at 32 h is cycling. If a 10 km run turns out to impair the next-day leg session badly, the rotated template still works (legs are maintenance) but the conservative alternative becomes relatively more attractive.
- **All separation/recovery RCTs (Robineau, Sale, Yang) are in lifters far less trained than him.** Repeated-bout protection means his post-leg-day soreness is likely smaller than theirs, which shrinks — not removes — the Tuesday-run problem.
- **If he were a lower-body strength athlete** the rotation recommendation would flip: consolidating legs the day after the long run would then cost a priority outcome and I would separate them ≥ 48 h in both directions and accept a worse week for the upper body.
- **If intake is already generous and body mass stable**, the energy-availability flag can be ignored.
- **If the running specialist changes the ladder's down-weeks**, recommendation 6's week numbers move with them; the principle (align deloads with down-weeks, race week = lifting maintenance) does not.
