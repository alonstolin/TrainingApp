# Hypertrophy (side delts, rear delts, biceps, triceps) — Research Findings

Status: complete.

Athlete context that drives every judgement below: 10+ years continuous
training, drug-free, near his natural ceiling, high sensitivity to volume and
recovery, four fixed lifting days, two runs. Almost none of the literature was
run on someone like him; where a finding rests on untrained subjects I flag it
and discount it.

## Summary

- **The RP MEV/MAV/MRV bands the program cites are expert opinion, not measured
  landmarks.** The 2025 Pelland meta-regression (35 hypertrophy studies, 1,032
  participants) finds a smooth, diminishing dose–response with *no* detectable
  plateau and no per-muscle landmarks; the minimum effective dose is ~4
  fractional sets/week and each extra detectable increment needs progressively
  more sets (~6 sets in the 5–10 band, ~8.5 in 11–18, ~10.75 in 19–29). The
  program's 8→14 direct sets sits in the "intermediate efficiency" band; nothing
  in the data says 14 is a ceiling or that 8 is a floor.
- **Fractional counting (indirect set = 0.5) is the best-supported way to count
  OHP/incline/rows/pull-ups toward delts, triceps and biceps** — it won the
  model comparison in Pelland 2025 with "strong/very strong" Bayes-factor
  support over both "count everything" and "count only direct". The 0.5 weight is
  an explicit heuristic, not a measured constant. Once you count OHP + incline
  bench fractionally, the athlete's *effective* triceps and front/side delt
  volume is already meaningfully above the "8 direct sets" the program reports.
- **Effort, not exercise selection, is where the recent literature has moved.**
  Robinson et al. 2024 meta-regression: hypertrophy rises continuously as sets
  approach failure; strength does not. But the best trained-subject RCT (Refalo
  2024, ≥3 y trained) found 1–2 RIR = failure for quad growth with markedly less
  fatigue. An RPE 9–10 target (0–1 RIR) on accessories is defensible; a hard
  RPE 10 on every set is not required and costs recovery he cannot spare.
- **Lengthened-position training is real but smaller than the internet says,
  and the only trained-subject RCT on arms found nothing.** Wolf et al. 2025
  (25 trained, 4.9 ± 4.1 y): lengthened partials vs full ROM across 8 upper-body
  exercises — biceps and triceps thickness identical (BF 0.16–0.39 for the
  null). Maeo 2023's "overhead = 1.5× long-head growth" is real but was in
  untrained subjects. The 2024/25 Varović regional meta-analysis (12 studies)
  finds trivial regional effects of muscle length (SMD 0.04–0.09). Practical
  read: keep one overhead triceps movement and full-ROM curls; do not chase
  exotic lengthened variants expecting a large payoff.
- **Cable vs dumbbell lateral raise does not matter** (Wolf/Schoenfeld group
  2025, 24 trained lifters, 7.1 y, 5 sets to failure 2×/wk: +3.3–4.6% either
  way, "extreme" Bayesian support for equivalence). Pick by joint comfort and
  loadability.
- **Frequency is volume-neutral for hypertrophy** at the volumes he runs
  (Schoenfeld 2019 meta, 25 studies; Pelland 2025: frequency slope 0.32%/session,
  CrI crosses zero). The per-session companion analysis (Remmert 2025) puts the
  point of undetectable superiority at ~11 fractional sets per muscle per
  session — so splitting 12–14 sets across two days is sensible but not
  because of a "recovery" mechanism the data can see.
- **A complete week off mid-block did not help or hurt hypertrophy** in the only
  deload RCT (Coleman 2024, n = 39, ~3–4 y trained) and slightly hurt strength.
  Evidence that a *ramp* (adding sets each week) beats steady volume is thin:
  Enes 2024 (trained males) showed a small, low-certainty hypertrophy benefit
  and a clearer strength benefit from adding sets every two weeks, but volumes
  there ran 22→52 sets/week on legs — a different world from this athlete.
- **Exercise order matters for strength on the lift done first and does not
  measurably change hypertrophy** (Nunes 2021 meta, 11 studies). Putting
  laterals before OHP will cost OHP load; putting them after costs nothing the
  literature can detect.
- **Maintenance volume is far below what the program spends on legs.** The
  classic data (Bickel 2011, untrained) held muscle at 1/9 of building volume
  in young adults; the 2025 dose–response puts the minimum *growth* dose at ~4
  fractional sets/week. Legs at 14+ direct sets/week is a growth dose competing
  with the long run, not maintenance. **[PARTIAL — trained-lifter maintenance
  evidence still being gathered]**

## Where the current program is wrong, unsupported, or suboptimal

1. **"Landing near the top of RP's MAV band in week 4"** — *unsupported as
   stated.* RP's per-muscle MEV/MAV/MRV numbers are a coaching heuristic
   (Israetel; tier 5 expert opinion). No published dose–response analysis
   subgroups by muscle to that granularity, and the largest one (Pelland 2025)
   finds no plateau to anchor an "MRV" on. The program's 8→14 direct sets is a
   reasonable *range* but the rationale is wrong; the numbers should be justified
   from the dose–response tiers and this athlete's recovery, not from RP's
   table. Confidence: **high** that the RP landmarks are not measured; **moderate**
   that 8→14 is nonetheless a sensible band for him.

2. **Counting only direct sets understates his real volume.** Fractional
   counting (Pelland 2025) means the 6+ weekly sets of OHP, 6+ of incline
   bench, ~6 of pull-ups and ~6 of rows/pulldowns contribute roughly +3 to
   triceps, +3–6 to side/front delts, +6 to biceps and +3 to rear delts *before
   any accessory*. For triceps and biceps the "8 direct" baseline is really
   ~11–14 fractional; week 4's "14 direct" is ~17–20 fractional. For a lifter
   with high fatigue sensitivity that is not a small correction. Confidence:
   **moderate** (the 0.5 weight is a heuristic, but the direction is certain).

3. **The exercise-selection rationale leans on stretch-mediated hypertrophy
   claims that the trained-subject data do not reproduce.** Incline curl,
   Bayesian curl, overhead extension and cable laterals are all fine choices,
   but the evidence that they beat their "shortened" counterparts is (a) from
   untrained subjects (Maeo 2023, Pedrosa 2023, Sato 2021), (b) contradicted in
   untrained subjects for biceps (Attarieh 2025; Frontiers 2026), and (c) null
   in the one trained-subject arms RCT (Wolf 2025). Confidence: **moderate-high**
   that selection among these variants is a second-order variable for him.

4. **Reverse pec deck and face pull have no longitudinal hypertrophy evidence
   at all** — only EMG (tier 4). That does not make them wrong (rear delts are
   hard to train any other way), but the program should not present them as
   evidence-based picks. Confidence: **high** that the evidence is EMG-only.

5. **The RPE cap and set ramp together push effort and volume up in the same
   week (week 4: +2 sets AND RPE 9).** Robinson 2024 says closer-to-failure sets
   grow more; Refalo 2024 says 1–2 RIR matches failure in trained lifters with
   less fatigue. For an athlete described as fatigue-sensitive, the safer design
   is: hold accessories at a fixed 1–2 RIR (RPE 8–9) all block and let the
   *last* set of each exercise go to 0 RIR, rather than stacking a volume peak
   on an effort peak. Confidence: **moderate**.

6. **The deload is "half the sets at 0.85 load" — plausible but unstudied.**
   The one RCT (Coleman 2024) tested a *complete* week off and found no
   hypertrophy cost and a small strength cost. Nothing shows a half-volume
   deload beats no deload for growth in a 4-week block. For this athlete the
   deload's value is as insurance against accumulated fatigue and for the
   running, not as a growth mechanism. Confidence: **moderate**.

7. **Leg volume is a growth dose labelled as maintenance.** Squat 3×5–8 + RDL
   3×8–10 + leg press 3×10–12 + leg curl 3×10–15 = 12 direct sets/week for quads
   and hamstrings, plus running. Maintenance data (Bickel 2011; Spiering 2021
   review) says muscle is held at a fraction of that. Confidence: **moderate**
   (maintenance data mostly untrained). **[PARTIAL]**

8. **Legs and pull volume placed in the same session with curls** is fine for
   hypertrophy (order does not change growth — Nunes 2021), but note the
   strength side: pull-up volume sets after squats/RDLs will lose reps. Outside
   my domain; noted for the strength-coach synthesis.

## Findings

### 1. Volume landmarks for an advanced lifter

**Finding.** The best current dose–response evidence (Pelland et al. 2025,
Sports Medicine) shows hypertrophy increasing with weekly fractional sets with
diminishing returns and *no identifiable plateau* inside the data (sparse
beyond ~25 fractional sets/week). Marginal gain at the dataset mean (12.25
fractional sets) is ~0.24% muscle size per additional weekly set. The "minimum
effective dose" is 4 fractional sets/week. Training status was adjusted for in
every model, but the authors say moderator analyses (including training
status) are "hypothesis generating" only and that "extreme caution is
warranted" — the data cannot tell you whether an advanced lifter's curve is
shifted. RP's MEV/MAV/MRV numbers do not appear in, and are not derived from,
any of these analyses; they are Israetel's coaching estimates (tier 5).

Trained-subject RCTs disagree with each other on whether more than ~12–16
sets/week helps: Aube 2022 (35 trained, 12 vs 18 vs 24 lower-body sets) found
no differences; Brigatto 2022 (trained men, 16 vs 24 vs 32) found 24 and 32 >
16; Schoenfeld 2019 (34 trained men, 1 vs 3 vs 5 sets/exercise, 3×/wk) found a
dose–response for size but not strength; Enes 2024 (trained males, 22 fixed vs
+4 or +6 sets every 2 weeks) found a "possible small benefit" for hypertrophy
with "limited certainty" and a clearer strength benefit. None of these subjects
were 10-year lifters near their ceiling; "trained" in this literature is
typically 2–5 years.

Direction for this athlete: the evidence gives no reason to believe he needs
*more* volume than an intermediate; it gives a specific reason (diminishing
returns with rising cost per detectable increment, and his stated fatigue
sensitivity) to sit in the efficient part of the curve — roughly 10–18
fractional sets per priority muscle per week — and to buy progress with effort,
load progression and exercise rotation rather than with sets 15–20.

### 2. Counting indirect volume

**Finding.** Fractional counting — each indirect set (a compound where the
muscle is "meaningfully trained but not the primary force generator") counts
0.5 — is the *empirically best-fitting* quantification in the 2025 Pelland
meta-regression, with "strong" to "very strong" Bayes-factor support over both
total (1.0) and direct-only (0) counting for hypertrophy and strength. The
authors are explicit that 0.5 "is still an assumption", that the right weight
"likely depends on … the specific exercise, the repetition range employed, and
the training status of the participants", and that it "should be regarded as a
heuristic". So: fractional counting is convention *with* meta-analytic support;
the exact 0.5 is not measured.

Direct RCT evidence on whether compounds grow the small muscles as well as
isolation work is untrained-only and mixed: Gentil 2013 (untrained; adding
curls/extensions to bench + pulldown added nothing to arm thickness, 6.5% vs
7.0%); Mannarino 2021 (untrained; curl 11.1% vs dumbbell row 5.2% elbow-flexor
growth in a within-subject design). For an advanced lifter I infer the
Mannarino direction is the safer one: rows and pull-ups are worth *something*
to biceps but well under a curl; OHP and incline bench are worth something to
triceps and front/side delts but well under an extension or a lateral raise.

Practical counts for his week (fractional): OHP 6–7 sets → +3–3.5 to side delts
and triceps; incline bench 6–7 → +3–3.5 to triceps and front delts; weighted
pull-ups 6–7 → +3–3.5 to biceps; rows + pulldown ~6 → +3 to biceps, +3 to rear
delts. Baseline effective weekly volume in week 1 is therefore roughly: side
delts ~11, triceps ~14–15, biceps ~14–15, rear delts ~9 — not 8/8/8/6.

  for the precise 0.5.

### 3. Exercise selection: lengthened-position and stretch-mediated hypertrophy

**Finding.** The field moved in three steps. 2021–2023: a run of mostly
untrained single-muscle studies showed more growth from training at long
muscle lengths (Maeo 2023 triceps long head 28.5% overhead vs 19.6% neutral;
Pedrosa 2023 preacher curl initial-ROM distal biceps d = 0.89 vs 0.23; Kassiano
2023 calf 15.2% vs 6.7%; Sato 2021 elbow-flexor long-length). 2024–2025:
meta-analytic pooling found the effect is real but *small* — a partial-rep
long-vs-short pooled ES ≈ 0.28 (8 studies, mostly quads), and the Varović
regional meta-analysis (12 studies) found *trivial* regional differences
(proximal SMD 0.04, mid 0.07, distal 0.09) with the caveat that the typical
contrast studied was only ~22% of muscle length. 2025–2026: the first trained-
subject, multi-exercise RCT (Wolf et al., 25 lifters, 4.9 y) found lengthened
partials = full ROM for biceps and triceps thickness; and two untrained-subject
biceps RCTs that specifically tested shoulder position (preacher vs Bayesian,
Attarieh 2025; neutral vs peak-extension cable curl, Frontiers 2026) found no
difference at all in any region.

What survives for this athlete:
- **Triceps:** train the long head under stretch (overhead cable/EZ extension)
  — the only lengthened-position result with a large effect and a plausible
  anatomical mechanism (biarticular long head). Keep one pressing-plane or
  pushdown movement for the lateral/medial heads; nothing shows head-specific
  targeting beyond overhead-vs-neutral. Confidence **moderate** (Maeo is
  untrained; Wolf 2025 trained found no advantage to *partials* at long length
  but did use overhead DB extensions in both arms).
- **Biceps:** the incline / Bayesian / preacher distinction is not supported by
  the 2025–2026 data. Full-ROM curls to near failure with progressive load are
  what the evidence rewards. Rotate variants for elbow health and novelty, not
  for "regional" growth. Confidence **moderate-high**.
- **Side delts:** cable vs dumbbell lateral raise is a wash in trained lifters
  (Wolf/Schoenfeld group 2025, 7.1 y, 8 wk to failure). No study has tested a
  lengthened-partial lateral raise on deltoid hypertrophy. Confidence **high**
  on equivalence; **low** on any lengthened variant.
- **Rear delts:** reverse pec deck (neutral grip) and face pulls are supported
  by EMG only (reverse fly > rows for posterior deltoid; neutral > pronated hand
  position). No training-study evidence exists for either. Confidence **low**
  (tier 4).

### 4. Frequency for accessories

**Finding.** Volume-equated, frequency has no detectable effect on hypertrophy.
Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019 (25 studies, 836 participants, I² = 0%) found
no difference between higher and lower frequencies once weekly sets were held
constant; a benefit for higher frequency appeared only when volume was *not*
equated (i.e., more frequency = more sets). Pelland 2025 modelled frequency
continuously and found a slope of 0.32% per session [95% CrI −0.14, 0.82] —
91.3% probability > 0 but "compatible with negligible effects". The companion
per-session analysis (Remmert et al. 2025) found the point of undetectable
outcome superiority at ~11 fractional sets per muscle per session (≈8 direct),
"not an upper limit" and "unclear whether additional per-session sets attenuate
adaptations". The authors speculate the missing frequency effect may partly
reflect that "the collective training status of the included participants was
not sufficiently advanced" — the MPS window is shorter in trained lifters
(returns to baseline ~24–48 h), which is the one mechanistic argument for a
frequency effect in *this* athlete; it is tier 4 and unproven.

For small muscles there is no separate evidence. Delts and arms in these
datasets are mostly measured as by-products of whole-body programs.

Direction: 12–14 direct sets split 2× (6–7 per session) is well inside the
per-session efficient zone; putting all 14 in one session would exceed the
~11 fractional PUOS once you add the indirect sets from OHP/incline on the same
day. Two exposures is the right call, for a per-session-volume reason rather
than a "frequency" reason. Confidence **moderate-high**.

### 5. Rep ranges and load

**Finding.** Load is not a hypertrophy lever once sets are taken near failure.
Schoenfeld 2017 (meta, 21 studies) and Lopez 2021 (network meta, 28 studies,
747 adults; >15RM vs 9–15RM vs ≤8RM to failure) both find no hypertrophy
difference between loads; strength favours heavy. The only trained-subject data
that report arms separately (Schoenfeld 2015, 18 trained men, 25–35 vs 8–12
reps to failure) found the *high-rep* group grew elbow flexors more (+8.6% vs
+5.3%) and elbow extensors the same (+5.2% vs +6.0%). So for small muscles
there is no advantage to 6–10 over 12–20; if anything the trained-arm data lean
the other way, though with n = 9 per group that is noise-level. The program's
12–15 / 15–20 for laterals and face pulls is therefore *permitted* by the
evidence, not *demanded* by it. The practical case for higher reps on laterals
(2.5 kg plate jumps are too coarse at 10–15 kg dumbbells; shoulder-joint
tolerance; less systemic fatigue per set) is coaching reasoning I endorse, not
data. What the evidence *does* demand at any rep range is proximity to failure
(Q6) — a 15–20-rep lateral raise stopped at 5 RIR is a wasted set.

Direction for this athlete: keep laterals and rear-delt work at 12–20, biceps
and triceps anywhere from 8–15 by exercise (heavier on preacher/pushdown,
lighter on overhead/Bayesian for elbow comfort), and progress via double
progression. There is no reason to add a "heavy 6–8" accessory slot.
Confidence **moderate-high**.

### 6. Proximity to failure

**Finding.** The picture changed between 2022 and 2024. Grgic 2022 (meta)
found no overall failure-vs-non-failure difference but a small significant
benefit of failure for hypertrophy in the *trained* subgroup (ES 0.15 [0.03,
0.26]). Refalo 2023 (systematic review, 15 studies) found "no evidence" that
momentary failure beats non-failure and suggested a non-linear relationship.
Robinson et al. 2024 then modelled RIR continuously across the literature
(adjusting for load, volume-equating method, duration and training status) and
found hypertrophy increases as sets approach failure while strength gain is
flat across a wide RIR range — but with wide uncertainty because RIR was
*estimated* from study descriptions, and with the explicit caveat that the
precise magnitude per RIR is unclear. The best trained-subject RCT (Refalo 2024:
18 lifters ≥3 y, leg press to failure vs 2-RIR and leg extension to failure vs
1-RIR, 8 wk) found identical quadriceps growth (0.181 vs 0.182 cm) with
consistently more velocity loss and rep loss in the failure limb.

Synthesis for this athlete: sets should finish within ~0–2 RIR; the last 1–2
RIR are worth little measurable growth in trained lifters and cost measurably
more fatigue. The program's "RPE 9–10 cap" (≈0–1 RIR) is at the aggressive end
of what the evidence supports. The failure-subgroup signal in Grgic 2022 is the
only reason not to back off to 2–3 RIR entirely. Confidence **moderate-high**.

### 7. Mesocycle design

**Finding.** Three separate questions, three different evidence levels.

(a) **Ramping volume across the block vs holding it flat.** No study tests a
4-week +1/+1/+2 ramp against flat volume. The nearest evidence points *against*
the ramp for someone at his volume: Enes JAP 2024 (trained ≥3 y, squat ≥1.5×BW)
had the control group keep its habitual ~14 quad sets/week while others jumped
+30% / +60% for 8 weeks — hypertrophy was the same and the *flat* group got
stronger; Moreno 2024 (recreationally trained, 12-wk curls) found adding sets
over baseline did not beat holding baseline; a 2026 within-subject JAP study
found a +120% jump neither helped nor hurt over 8 weeks. Enes MSSE 2024 is the
one ramp-positive study and it started at 22 sets and climbed toward 52 — a
context that does not exist for this athlete. Periodization meta-analyses
(Grgic 2017; Moesgaard 2022, volume-equated) find periodization helps strength
(in trained lifters especially, favouring undulating) but not hypertrophy. So:
volume ramping is a strength/periodization habit, not a hypertrophy mechanism.
Confidence **moderate**.

(b) **Deloads.** Coleman 2024 (n = 39, ≥1 y trained, 20 sets/muscle/week, 4
sessions) gave a complete week off in week 5 of 9: no hypertrophy difference,
slightly worse strength, and participants reported feeling lethargic rather than
refreshed. A 2026 within-subject replication in untrained men (Sci Rep) exists
but I could not open it. No study tests a *reduced-volume* deload vs none, and
none tests one in a high-fatigue advanced lifter running concurrently. Expert
consensus (Bell et al. Delphi; Rogerson 2024 survey of 246 competitive
strength/physique athletes, 8.2 y training) is: deload ~every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks,
lasting ~6.4 ± 1.7 days, cut volume 30–60% (moderate recovery needs) via fewer
sets, keep frequency and exercise selection, reduce load and effort (more RIR)
rather than stopping. Bell also notes "the risk of non-functional overreaching
following prolonged high-volume RT without deloading is low", i.e. frequent
proactive deloads are insurance, not a driver. Confidence **moderate** for
"deload does not build muscle"; **low** for optimal structure (consensus only).

(c) **Reducing volume across a block for a fatigue-sensitive lifter.** No direct
evidence. Two indirect lines support a *flat-or-slightly-descending* structure:
the trained-subject volume RCTs above (holding volume ≥ adding it over 8–12
weeks), and Refalo 2024's finding that the cost of the last 1–2 RIR is fatigue,
not growth. The classic "summated microcycle" (rise, rise, rise, drop) is a
strength-sport convention (Bell 2025 review) that hypertrophy data do not
require. I am **inferring**, not reporting, that for this athlete a block of
flat accessory volume with effort held at 1–2 RIR and one hard week (week 4 at
0–1 RIR) followed by a 40–50% volume deload is at least as good as the current
ramp and cheaper in fatigue. Block length 5–6 weeks is consistent with the
survey mean and with nothing in the RCTs arguing for shorter.

### 8. Exercise order

**Finding.** Nunes et al. 2021 (systematic review + meta-analysis, 11
good-to-excellent-quality studies) found strength gains largest in the exercise
done first, and no effect of order on hypertrophy whether multi-joint or
single-joint led, combining site-specific and indirect measures. The underlying
RCTs are mostly untrained (e.g., Avelar 2019, Trindade 2019), so the hypertrophy
null carries a transfer caveat — but there is no trained-subject evidence
pointing the other way.

For this athlete: laterals or curls placed before OHP/pull-ups will reduce the
load he can lift on the strength goal; placed after, the accessory does the
same job for growth. Where a priority muscle *also* gets its work from a main
lift (side delts from OHP), the main lift first is the correct order. The
optional Thursday delt/arm day is the one place priority muscles can go first
at no cost.

Evidence:
- Nunes JP et al. 2021. What influence does resistance exercise order have on
  muscular strength gains and muscle hypertrophy? *Eur J Sport Sci* 21(2):149–57.
  https://doi.org/10.1080/17461391.2020.1733672 · systematic review +
  meta-analysis · 11 studies · mostly untrained · strength ↑ in first exercise;
  hypertrophy no effect · **confidence moderate** (untrained-dominated;
  direction unlikely to reverse in trained).

### 9. Specialisation

**Finding.** No RCT directly compares "prioritise 2–4 muscle groups,
maintain the rest" against a balanced program in trained lifters; the
specialisation logic is expert consensus (Israetel, Helms, Nuckols) built on
two evidenced pieces: (i) the volume dose–response has diminishing returns, so
concentrating sets where you want growth is efficient; (ii) maintenance needs
far less than building. On (ii): Bickel 2011 (untrained young adults, 16 wk of
27 leg sets/wk then 32 wk at 1/9 volume = 3 sets/wk at 8–12RM) fully maintained
muscle size in young subjects; Spiering 2021 (review) endorses roughly one-third
frequency and one-third sets as a maintenance dose for muscle size in trained
and untrained; Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 (review, trained powerlifters) puts
the minimum *strength-gaining* dose at 1 hard set, 2–3×/week per lift at
70–85% 1RM. Pelland 2025's minimum *growth* dose is 4 fractional sets/week.

Inference for this athlete: chest and back are already covered by the strength
lifts (incline bench, weighted pull-up) and need no extra hypertrophy work.
Legs at 12 direct sets/week is a building dose; ~4–6 hard sets/week (one squat
or hack pattern, one hinge) would hold what he has and free recovery for the
run. Confidence **moderate** (maintenance data largely untrained; direction
robust).

## Recommendations for this athlete

1. **Count volume fractionally and report it that way.** Weekly effective sets
   (direct + 0.5 × indirect) per priority muscle should target: side delts
   12–16, rear delts 10–14, triceps 12–16, biceps 12–16. With the main lifts
   contributing ~3–6 fractional sets to each, that is roughly **8–12 direct
   accessory sets/week** in the working weeks, not 14. Do not exceed ~11
   fractional sets for one muscle in a single session (count the OHP sets on
   the same day).
2. **Do not ramp volume and effort together.** Hold accessory sets at RPE 8–9
   (1–2 RIR) for the block; take only the final set of each accessory to RPE
   10. If a ramp is kept, ramp one variable: sets +1/week at constant RPE, or
   RPE 8→9 at constant sets — not both.
3. **Exercise selection:** side delts — any lateral raise he can
   load progressively (cable and DB equivalent), full ROM; rear delts — reverse
   pec deck neutral grip + a face pull or rear-delt row (EMG-only support);
   triceps — one overhead extension (long head) + one pushdown/pressing-plane
   movement; biceps — two full-ROM curls with different resistance profiles,
   progressed on load; the incline/Bayesian/preacher labels are interchangeable
   on current evidence.
4. **Keep two exposures per priority muscle per week** (per-session volume
   reason), with the priority accessories placed *after* the main lift of the
   day, and the optional Thursday used for a third, low-fatigue exposure
   (laterals, rear delts, curls) when running recovery allows.
5. **Legs to true maintenance:** one squat/hack pattern 2–3 sets + one hinge
   2–3 sets per week, hard (1–2 RIR), ~5–6 sets total. Drop leg press or leg
   curl to free recovery for the long run. Chest and back: no accessory
   hypertrophy work beyond rows/pulldown already present for the pull-up.
6. **Deload:** keep a reduced week every 4–6 weeks for fatigue and running,
   but treat it as a light week (half sets, same load or −10%) rather than
   expecting it to produce growth; the evidence says it will neither help nor
   hurt size.
7. **Rep ranges:** laterals and rear-delt work at 12–20 reps; biceps and
   triceps 8–15 reps depending on exercise (heavier on preacher/pushdown-style
   movements, lighter on overhead/Bayesian variants for elbow comfort). Load
   is not a hypertrophy lever once sets are near failure, so choose ranges for
   joint comfort and plate-jump granularity, not for a size advantage, and
   progress every accessory by double progression. There is no case for a
   heavy 6–8 accessory slot.
8. **Mesocycle length and shape:** run a 5–6 week block — flat accessory
   volume and effort per #1–#2 through the working weeks, then the deload in
   #6 — rather than the current ramp of both sets and RPE together. Nothing in
   the trained-lifter volume RCTs favours ramping over flat volume for growth;
   ramping is a strength/periodization habit, not a hypertrophy requirement.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions

- **Trained volume RCTs conflict**: Aube 2022 (12 = 18 = 24) vs Brigatto 2022
  (24, 32 > 16) vs Schoenfeld 2019 (dose–response 1→5 sets). The meta-regression
  smooths this into "diminishing returns, no plateau", but the residual spread
  is large (R²marginal 22%). Individual response dominates at his level; the
  practical answer is to autoregulate within 10–18 fractional sets.
- **Effort**: Robinson 2024 (closer to failure = more growth, continuous) vs
  Refalo 2024 (1–2 RIR = failure in trained subjects). Both can be true if the
  curve is steep between 5 and 2 RIR and flat between 2 and 0. That is what I
  assume; it has not been tested directly in advanced lifters.
- **Lengthened partials**: untrained single-muscle studies (Maeo, Pedrosa,
  Kassiano) vs trained multi-exercise null (Wolf 2025) and untrained biceps
  nulls (Attarieh 2025; Frontiers 2026). The likely reconciliation is that
  muscle-length effects are muscle-specific (triceps long head and calves yes;
  biceps no) and shrink with training status. If the athlete's triceps long
  head is a specific weakness, overhead work is the one place the evidence
  still pays.
- **Nothing here was measured in a 10-year lifter.** "Trained" in this
  literature is 2–5 years. If he responds to volume better than expected, the
  fractional target can rise toward 18; if fatigue rises first, it should fall
  toward 10.
- **Face pull / reverse pec deck**: zero longitudinal evidence. Open question
  whether rear delts even need direct work beyond rows and pull-ups at his
  level; the fractional model would credit rows with half a set each.
- **Running interaction** (outside my domain, load-bearing here): the case for
  cutting leg volume to maintenance rests on the long run supplying leg stress;
  if the running coach finds leg strength work is *protective* for the 10K
  progression, keep the squat pattern but still cut the accessory leg sets.
