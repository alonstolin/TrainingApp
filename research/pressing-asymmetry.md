# Pressing asymmetry (standing barbell OHP) — Research Findings

I am not a clinician. This is a framework for deciding what to measure and whether to change a programme. It is not a diagnosis, I have not seen the footage, and nothing here establishes that the athlete has an asymmetry. Where this file says "refer", it means it.

**Access note, because it bears on trust.** Most primary papers here could not be opened as full text (PMC served a captcha, Nature and JOSPT returned 403/redirects, PubMed wanted cookies). Where a line says "abstract", I read the abstract via the Europe PMC API and not the methods. Where it says "full page", I read the article. Where it says "search summary", I only saw a search-engine paraphrase and the claim is weaker than it looks. Nothing below is cited from memory without being labelled.

## Summary

- **The intuitive answer ("he has an imbalance, add unilateral work") does not survive scrutiny as stated.** Three links in it are each unproven: that the difference is real, that it is a strength difference, and that unilateral work fixes a bilateral lift. I found no trial in any population that tests whether reducing a pressing asymmetry improves bilateral pressing strength, hypertrophy or upper-body injury risk. The asymmetry literature is associative, mostly lower-limb, and its own reviews say the thresholds are unsupported (Parkinson 2021; Bishop 2018).
- **A visible side-to-side difference in a bilateral lift is the expected finding, not the exception.** Dominant upper limbs are on average ~11.6% stronger across 87 studies (Foley 2025, mostly grip and isolated joints, general population). In n = 10 trained bodybuilders, symmetric bench pressing gave dominant pec 51 vs 35 %MVIC (Frontiers 2025). In n = 21 men with 5+ years of bench experience, 4 weeks of ordinary bench training grew the dominant pec more (0.20 vs 0.13 cm). So "he has some asymmetry" is nearly certain and carries almost no information. What matters is whether it is big, replicable, progressive, painful, or limiting the lift.
- **Establishing the asymmetry is harder than it looks, and the measurement noise is the same size as the thresholds people quote.** Test-retest CV for upper-body 1RM is a median 4% (Grgic 2020). Asymmetry indices built from two such tests have a 95% minimal detectable change near 8–21% (Zöger 2026; Degot 2021 reports 12.4% for the seated shot-put LSI; my own arithmetic gives ~11% for a single-session comparison at CV 4%). A 5% single-session difference is not a finding.
- **Observers see what they expect.** Visual rating of scapular dyskinesis has kappa 0.12 among 104 physiotherapists, and ratings rose with how much the rater believed in the construct (Vila-Dieguez 2026). The athlete's two observations (an OHP video, a physique video) are not independent: the second was read with the first in mind, and the project's own physique work found frames cannot separate posture from muscle size (`research/physique-metrics.md`).
- **The "overactive upper trap / scapular upward-rotation asymmetry" story is largely folklore at the level of this athlete.** A 22-study EMG review found most muscle comparisons in shoulder pain were absent or contradictory; only serratus anterior had limited support for lower, delayed activation (Kinsella 2017). Dyskinesis was not more common in 67 shoulder-pain patients than in 68 controls (Plummer 2017). The one pooled prospective estimate (+43% relative risk of shoulder pain, 5 studies, n = 419 athletes, Hickey 2018) is associative and in athletes unlike him.
- **Barbell OHP stays.** It is a named goal, the engine's reference lives on the heavy day, and strength gains follow specificity (Kassiano 2025 meta-analysis: hypertrophy equal, bilateral strength better after bilateral training). If anything is swapped, it should be the Wednesday volume-day OHP, which does not feed the heavy-day reference and can be replaced one-for-one without adding volume.
- **What I would do first costs nothing in the gym:** re-film properly with a defined protocol (Section 2), look for the asymmetry in unloaded, light and heavy conditions, check the grip marks, and do a two-day single-arm rep test on a rest day. Act only if the result clears the noise, or if there is pain.

## 1. Differential diagnosis

An observation is not a cause. Each row says what else must be true if that cause is the one. The right-hand column is the cheapest observation that separates it from its neighbours. Evidence status is in the last column: "convention" means clinical or coaching logic with no published test of the discriminating rule.

| # | Candidate cause | What else would be true | Cheapest discriminator | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Not real: camera or observer artefact** (parallax, off-centre camera, lens tilt, expectation) | Difference changes or reverses when the camera moves; absent from a rear view; a second person watching live does not see it; bar tilt reads ~0 with a level on the bar | Re-film at bar height, ~3 m, front and rear; flip the clip horizontally; put a phone level or tape line on the bar | Observer bias is documented (Vila-Dieguez 2026). Protocol is convention |
| 1 | **Setup: grip or bar placement** (hands off the knurl marks, bar off-centre on the shoulders or rack, wrist angle) | Visible with an empty bar; changes the moment hands are re-set to the ring marks; present in every lift, not only heavy sets | Mark the centre, set hands to the same distance from the marks, film again | Inference. Free to check and common |
| 2 | **Unilateral strength deficit** (delt, triceps, upper pec) | Bar tilt or lag grows with load and with proximity to failure; none at empty bar; single-arm rep-max differs on both of two separate days in the same direction; weaker side usually the non-dominant one (a prior, not proof) | Compare film at empty bar, ~60%, and the last rep of a heavy back-off; two-day single-arm test | Dominance prior: Foley 2025. Test thresholds: convention |
| 3 | **Overhead mobility restriction** (glenohumeral, lat or pec/pec minor length, thoracic extension or rotation, rib position) | Present at empty-bar loads; same compensation every rep regardless of load (lumbar arch, lateral lean, elbow flare); active and passive range both limited on that side with a firm stop; wall test shows one arm lagging | Active vs passive shoulder flexion with ribs and lower back flat to a wall, smartphone inclinometer, both sides | Inclinometer reliability is documented (below). That mobility limits OHP is the field's belief, supported at tier 6 only |
| 4 | **Scapulothoracic motor control** (serratus anterior, trapezius balance) | Passive range fine, active elevation worse on one side; difference visible unloaded or with a 1–2 kg weight; improves when the scapula is manually assisted or cued; may show winging on a push-up plus | Unloaded and light weighted elevation filmed from behind; wall push-up plus | Visual rating unreliable (kappa 0.12–0.61); see Section 6 |
| 5 | **Trunk asymmetry** (structural scoliosis, rib prominence, rotation or side-flexion preference) | Lateral lean present in every overhead task, including an unloaded reach; positive forward-bend test; visible in plain standing photos | Forward-bend (Adams) test; filmed unloaded overhead reach | Adams test reliability is adolescent-screening evidence only; flag as transfer |
| 6 | **Leg and hip drive or stance** (weight shift, one-sided knee kick, foot placement) | Appears only standing; disappears or shrinks seated with back support; foot pressure or heel lift differs between sides; history of lower-limb injury | Seated, back-supported press (dumbbells) vs standing barbell, same film protocol | Inference |
| 7 | **Learned bar-path habit** | Long-standing and never noticed; present at empty bar; changes within a set when given a cue, mirror or live video; varies between sessions | Re-film with a "level the bar" visual cue; does it self-correct? | Inference. Feedback does improve lifting output and technique in general (Weakley 2023 meta-analysis, 20 studies), but not this specific fault |
| 8 | **Handedness / dominance** | Not a separate cause but a prior: direction of any difference matches the dominant side; magnitude in the single digits to low teens | Direction check against hand dominance | Foley 2025, dominant 11.6% stronger (range 2.1–19.5%) |
| 9 | **Prior injury or structural** (AC joint, labrum, cuff, clavicle fracture, neck) | History; a painful arc, catching, apprehension, or loss of a specific range; side difference in rotation ROM; often onset can be dated | History first; any positive item goes to Section 7 | Clinical, not programmable |
| 10 | **Neurological or muscular** (long thoracic, spinal accessory, cervical root) | Visible wasting or winging at rest; weakness out of proportion; sensory symptoms; trapezius contour change or drooping shoulder | Section 7 | StatPearls (full page): serratus palsy hard at >120° elevation, trapezius palsy droops and winges with abduction |

Two things in this table do real work. First, rows 0, 1 and 7 are free and common, and they are the ones the intuitive answer skips. Second, the load-dependence test separates the families cleanly: **a difference present at empty bar is structure, mobility, control or habit; a difference that appears only as the bar gets heavy or the rep gets slow is capacity.** I could not find a published test of that rule for OHP, so it is a convention, and the cleanest one available.

One further discriminator the athlete already has: **was it there before today?** A pattern he has pressed with for 10+ years and only noticed on film is far more likely a stable habit or dominance effect than something new. A new, progressive or painful change is a different problem (Section 7).

## 2. Assessment that distinguishes them

All of this is done alone, at home or in a commercial gym, with no per-side logging in the programme. Everything is one-off.

### 2a. Re-film, with a protocol (10 minutes, then keep the clips)

- Phone at bar height, about 3 m away, level (use the phone's grid), front view and a separate rear view. Same lighting, same footwear. No cropping above the head or below the knees.
- Conditions in this order: empty bar x5; a light set (~50–60% of the heavy-day working weight) x5; the last back-off set on a normal Sunday, filmed unprompted. Do not tell yourself which side to look at before you watch.
- Put a phone spirit-level app flat on the bar for the first two conditions, or run a horizontal tape line behind the lifter, so bar tilt is read from a reference and not judged by eye.
- Also film: an unloaded two-arm overhead reach, and a standing resting shot front and back. These separate row 5 (trunk) from the lift itself.
- **Noise yardstick:** film the same condition twice, minutes apart, re-setting the camera between. If the side difference between the two takes is as big as the difference you are trying to explain, you have found nothing. This is the same within-session spread logic `research/physique-metrics.md` recommends for tape, and it is a convention, not a validated rule.

**What counts as positive:** bar tilt or lockout difference that (a) is the same direction across both takes and both views, (b) exceeds the take-to-take spread, and (c) tells you which load condition it first appears at.

### 2b. Strength: a two-day single-arm test (a one-off bilateral comparison)

- Seated, back-supported single-arm dumbbell press (this removes legs, trunk lean and balance). Pick a dumbbell that gives roughly 8 reps to near failure on the weaker-feeling side. Do one set per side to the same end-point (last clean rep, bar speed visibly slowing), strong side first or second is arbitrary, randomise it.
- Repeat on a second day at least 3 days later, away from heavy pressing (Thursday is the free day). Count reps at the fixed load.
- **Use reps, not load, as the unit.** Dumbbell racks jump in 2 kg steps, which is 7–10% of a 20–30 kg dumbbell, too coarse to resolve a 5% difference. One rep at 6–10 reps is about 3% of e1RM, which is a finer scale (arithmetic from the app's own Epley relationship, `coach/DECISIONS.md` also uses 1 rep ≈ 3%).
- **Positive:** the same side loses by 2 or more reps on both days. One day alone is not a finding.
- **Why 2 reps and two days, stated plainly:** upper-body 1RM test-retest CV has a median of 4.0% (Grgic 2020, systematic review of 32 studies, n = 1,595, ICC median 0.97 for upper body). A comparison of two independent measures each with 4% error has a standard deviation of about 5.7%, so the 95% range is about +/-11% on a single occasion, and about +/-8% if the difference is averaged over two days. This is my arithmetic, assumes independent errors, and uses a barbell-1RM CV for a dumbbell rep test whose true CV is not published. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude argument for why a single-session 5% gap means nothing.
- Cross-check from independent literature: the unilateral seated shot-put LSI has a 95% MDC of 12.4% (Degot 2021, n = 27 male athletes, abstract), and isometric/isokinetic leg-press asymmetry has an MDC of 8.1–20.8% with the dominant side flipping between conditions in most people (Zöger 2026, n = 25 recreationally active adults, abstract). Both are non-pressing tasks and the second is lower body, so they support the order of magnitude and not the number.
- **Not recommended:** a bilateral test that tries to split force between hands (for example, two bathroom scales under a push-up). I could not find any published reliability for it, and it measures load distribution under a fixed posture rather than strength. If used, it is a curiosity, not a measurement.

### 2c. Mobility (10 minutes)

- **Shoulder flexion against a wall** (standing, heels 10 cm out, ribs down and lower back flat to the wall, raise each arm in the sagittal plane, thumb up), measured with a smartphone inclinometer on the upper arm. Take the best of three per side, active, then passive (a partner or the other hand over-pressing gently). Reported reliability: digital inclinometer intra-rater ICCs high and MDC 4–9 degrees; smartphone-application MDC 2.9–4.7 degrees; inter-rater flexion ICC 0.83–0.84 (search summaries of several studies, healthy adults, unverified primary). **Positive:** side difference larger than your own repeat spread and larger than ~5 degrees; active shorter than passive points to control or strength, active equal to passive with a firm stop points to range.
- **Pectoralis minor length** (supine, acromion height above the table, ruler or caliper). Borstad: intra-rater ICC 0.86–0.97 but inter-rater only 0.64–0.72 in subjects with and without shoulder symptoms (search summary). Use it only as the same person comparing sides. **Positive:** side difference beyond the repeat spread. No normative cut-off exists; convention.
- **Latissimus dorsi length: do not use as a discriminator.** In a 56-subject reliability study the ICC was 0.55–0.76 within raters and 0.33 between raters (SAJP, search summary of abstract). The overhead-reach-with-ribs-down test in the wall flexion above captures what you actually care about.
- **Thoracic rotation and extension:** seated rotation to each side, hands crossed, compare end-range by eye on film. I could not find a validated home measure; convention.
- **Hard stop vs soft stop:** passive end-feel is the standard clinical separator of a mechanical from a neural/control limit. Its reliability is poor and I did not research it further. Use only as a prompt.

### 2d. Motor control (the weakest category, said plainly)

- The named scapular tests have poor reliability in the people most likely to be rated. McClure 2009 (n = 142 NCAA overhead athletes, six raters, trained on a standard protocol): weighted kappa 0.48–0.61. Uhl 2009 (n = 56): yes/no kappa 0.40, four-type 0.44. Vila-Dieguez 2026 (104 physiotherapists, 5 videos of healthy shoulders): Fleiss kappa 0.12. A 2020 systematic review of 14 asymptomatic-subject studies concluded there were no qualitative methods with high reliability fit for clinical use (Paraskevopoulos 2020, abstract).
- So the useful motor-control tests are **task variations, not named tests**: does the difference shrink seated; at light load; with a single level-bar cue; one arm at a time; unloaded? A difference that melts under a cue and returns when you stop thinking is habit or control. A difference that is unchanged by every variation is more likely structure, range or capacity.
- A partner-assisted scapular test (the Scapular Assistance Test) exists with published intertester reliability, but I only saw its title in search results, not its results, so I am not endorsing it.

### 2e. Decision after assessment

| Result | Reading | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing clears the noise on any test, no pain | No established asymmetry | Do nothing. Re-film once per 4-week block under the same protocol. Close the question |
| Tilt present at empty bar, grip marks off | Setup | Fix the setup. Re-film |
| Difference at empty bar, range test positive | Mobility | Section 4b |
| Difference only under load; single-arm test positive on both days | Strength | Section 4a |
| Difference unloaded, changes with cues, tests otherwise equal | Control or habit | Cueing; see 4c |
| Any item in Section 7 | Clinician | Stop and refer |

## 3. Does it need fixing at all?

**Finding: I cannot show that it does. The assumption that a sub-clinical pressing asymmetry should be corrected is repeated by the field and is not supported by controlled evidence in any population I searched.**

### 3a. What is normal

| Source | Design / n | Population | Finding | Confidence for him |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foley et al. 2025, *Sci Rep* (abstract) | Scoping review + meta-analysis, 87 studies | General and mixed adults; heavily grip | Right 6.7% stronger than left (n = 9,342); dominant 11.6% stronger than non-dominant (n = 9,327); range by joint/movement 2.1–19.5% | Moderate that single digits to low teens is normal; low for pressing in trained lifters |
| Strength asymmetry study cited in `physique-metrics.md` (PMC9319678, read by that agent) | Cross-sectional, n = 55 | Physically active adults | 3–6% mean dominant advantage at the elbow; >65% asymmetric beyond a smallest-worthwhile-change on at least one metric; direction muscle-specific | Moderate for "normal"; not trained lifters |
| Frontiers in Physiology 2025 (full page) | Randomised crossover, acute, n = 10 | Collegiate male bodybuilders, 4.55 y training, 1RM bench 120 kg | Symmetric 70% 1RM bench: dominant pec 51 vs 35 %MVIC, anterior delt 48 vs 33 %MVIC | Low-moderate. sEMG with MVIC normalisation, n = 10 |
| Offset vs traditional loading, bench press (*J Sci Sport Exerc* 2022 and a companion paper; search summaries; authors not confirmed) | Parallel groups, 4-week mesocycle, n = 20–21 | Men, 5+ years bench experience, bench 1RM at or above bodyweight | Traditional loading grew dominant pec more than non-dominant (0.20 vs 0.13 cm); offset loading removed the difference | Low-moderate. 4 weeks, ultrasound, small n, unclear independence of the two papers |
| Parkinson et al. 2021, *Sports Med* review (abstract) | Systematic review, 53 articles | Mixed | Asymmetry ranges from near-zero to >15%; 15 of 18 studies that applied a threshold used 10–15% "often without supporting evidence"; only 4 of 12 indices avoid reference-limb bias | High that the 10–15% rule is a convention |
| Zöger et al. 2026 (abstract) | Test-retest, n = 25 | Recreationally active, full-body lifting | Mean asymmetry small (-3.5% to +1.1%), MDC 8.1–20.8%, dominance consistent across all conditions in only 16% | Moderate for noise; lower-body task |
| Degot et al. 2021 (abstract) | Test-retest, n = 27 | Male athletes | Seated shot-put LSI: ICC 0.82, SEM 0.045, MDC95 0.124 | Moderate; non-barbell upper-limb task |

The picture: in an unselected population a single-digit-to-low-teens difference is ordinary, the direction usually favours the dominant side, and **bilateral training does not equalise it and may drift it slightly the other way.** I found no published normative range for bar tilt, lockout height difference or lag between hands in a barbell OHP, so there is no benchmark for "how visible is normal" on film.

### 3b. Does correction improve anything?

| Claim the field makes | What I found | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Asymmetry hurts performance | Bishop 2018 systematic review (18 articles): strength differences "may be detrimental to jumping, kicking and cycling", mixed elsewhere, **all associative, no RCTs**. Fox 2023 meta-analysis (11 studies, lower-limb): weak negative association with change-of-direction and sprint, none with vertical jump | Low; no upper-body or bilateral-press data |
| Asymmetry predicts injury | Guan 2022 systematic review, 28 prospective studies, lower limb: "highly inconsistent findings"; Parkinson 2021: larger asymmetries "often" correlate with injury, "evidence remained inconsistent" | Low; lower-limb, young athletes |
| Upper-limb asymmetry predicts shoulder injury | Tooth 2020 (25 prospective studies, overhead/throwing athletes): modifiable risk factors are range of motion, rotator-cuff weakness and training load; scapular dysfunction "remains controversial" | Low-moderate; throwers, not barbell pressers, and the factors are absolute deficits and load, not side-to-side differences |
| Correcting asymmetry improves bilateral strength | **No trial found** in any population | Absence after targeted searches |
| Correcting asymmetry reduces injury | **No trial found** in any population with upper-limb or pressing outcomes | Absence |
| Correcting asymmetry improves hypertrophy | The offset-loading result above shows a size-gap closing over 4 weeks; no evidence this improves overall growth or that the gap is a problem | Low |
| Limb symmetry thresholds are valid | The project already found the 90% LSI is an unvalidated consensus convention derived in ACL populations (`research/physique-metrics.md`, PMC11874420, n = 233, no association with the outcome it predicts). Nothing here changes that; Parkinson 2021 says the same of the 10–15% rule more broadly | High that thresholds are convention |

### 3c. The argument against importing a rehabilitation threshold

The 90% LSI and the 10–15% rules were built to decide return-to-sport after surgery or for screening in team-sport athletes. The outcome was re-injury or jump/sprint performance, the tissue was usually lower limb, and the population was young. His outcome is a bilateral overhead press, in a healthy man with a decade of training, where the bar is held by both hands at once. Nothing supports carrying a number across.

What I use instead is a **measurement-noise rule**, not a health threshold: don't act on a difference you cannot reproduce on two days. The 8–12% MDC figures are the order of magnitude; the number itself is a convention and I label it so.

### 3d. Reasons that survive for acting anyway

1. **He wants shoulder hypertrophy and the asymmetry is visible.** A cosmetic goal is a legitimate reason to care even if performance does not need it. But I found no valid way to measure shoulder or trap size asymmetry at home (tape is reliable on arm and calf, not deltoid or trapezius; frames confound posture, `research/physique-metrics.md`). So the size claim cannot currently be established.
2. **A movement fault might cost him load on a lift he is trying to add load to.** That is plausible and testable (Section 2), but the OHP reference is a monotone engine variable; if the bar tilts at the top of every heavy back-off set, the log will show it as stalled progress long before any film does. That is a stronger monitor than a side-count.
3. **Pain or a Section 7 item.** That overrides everything.

If none of those three applies, my answer is that it does not need fixing.

## 4. If correction is warranted: what actually works

### 4a. Strength deficit confirmed

What the evidence supports, with honesty about its grade:

- **Unilateral exercises reduce side-to-side differences in unilateral tasks.** Evidence is lower-limb and athletic. A 10-week RCT in n = 30 university basketball players gave the non-dominant leg 3 sets and the dominant leg 1 (three sessions weekly, split squats, Bulgarian split squats, step-ups, single-leg calf raises, plus plyometrics). Single-leg jump asymmetry fell 15.25% to 7.86% (height) and isometric mid-thigh pull asymmetry 9.07% to 3.20% against 10.00% to 10.20% in controls (Frontiers in Physiology 2024, full page; no power analysis, small cohort). **The outcome is the same unilateral task trained, so this tells you unilateral training changes unilateral-task asymmetry, not that bilateral performance improves.** A smaller handball RCT (n = 30, 6 weeks, highly trained women) and a basketball study agree in direction (search summaries). No upper-limb equivalent found.
- **Unilateral and bilateral training produce the same hypertrophy and strength follows specificity.** Kassiano 2025 (systematic review and meta-analysis, 9 studies, abstract): no difference in muscle growth; bilateral training gave the larger gain in bilateral strength, unilateral in unilateral strength. This is the main argument against replacing the barbell, and for substituting rather than adding if you substitute at all.
- **Dumbbell and barbell pressing transfer to each other.** Smoak 2023 (*J Strength and Performance*; randomised, n = 30 resistance-trained men, mean age 18.1, 12 weeks, 2 sessions/week): no group-by-time interaction for barbell 1RM, dumbbell 1RM or seated medicine-ball throw. Young and short, so "trained" is generous. Confidence in transfer to a 10-year lifter's OHP: low-moderate.
- **Cross-education claims are weaker than repeated.** Wong 2024 (abstract, meta-analysis of RCTs, only 3 studies eligible): training the dominant limb did not clearly transfer more strength than training the non-dominant (pooled 0.46, SE 0.42). So "train the weak side and the strong side benefits too" and "train the dominant side and it transfers" are both thin.

**The "train the weak side first, match the strong side to it" convention.** I found no trial of that ordering or of matching-down. What exists is (i) the extra-volume-on-the-weak-side design above (3:1 sets), which is not "match down", and (ii) offset-loading and asymmetric-bar designs, which are acute EMG studies (Saeterbakken 2020, n = 17 trained men, 5% and 10% offset: the lighter side loses pec and shoulder activation at 10%; Frontiers 2025, n = 10, 2–4% offset described as best) or the 4-week ultrasound study above. None is a controlled test of the convention. **Verdict: a coaching convention. Reasonable because it is cheap and it protects the weaker side from being trained to the strong side's pace. Not evidence-based.** The first-side effect is probably nil when sides are alternated with rest in between, and I would not make a rule of it.

**Dose, honestly:** the only RCT dose I can point to (3 sets weak : 1 set strong, 3 sessions/week, 10 weeks, lower body, athletes) is incompatible with flat volume (SYNTHESIS 2.1) and has no upper-body equivalent. I recommend nothing beyond equal sets and equal reps per side. If the athlete wants the extra-volume version, that is an approved-decision change and the reviewer should see it.

**Time course:** the RCTs run 6–10 weeks; hypertrophy asymmetry shifted in 4 weeks in one small study; I found nothing on how long a *visible bar-path* difference takes to change. Set the gate at one 4-week block (re-film, repeat the two-day test) and a second at 8–10 weeks. If nothing has moved by then, the cause was probably not strength.

**Variants, and how they differ** (all evidence below is EMG or acute and does not show a better outcome):

| Variant | What it asks of the lifter | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Single-arm dumbbell press, seated, back-supported | Strict per-side strength; trunk resists rotation and side-flexion if standing | Cleanest strength comparison; most direct test of a deficit |
| Single-arm dumbbell press, standing | Adds oblique and hip contribution | Closer to the barbell, but mixes strength with trunk control, which confuses the diagnosis |
| Single-arm landmine press | Angled path, more scapular upward-rotation demand at the top, arm-supported by a fixed pivot | Often used in shoulders that dislike a straight overhead path; I found no comparative outcome data |
| Kettlebell press | Offset load, wrist and forearm demand | Barbell vs kettlebell EMG exists (prime mover and stabiliser differences, small n); outcome data absent |
| Alternating press | Each arm gets a rest; less per-arm fatigue | Weak side may be spared; makes the comparison less clean |
| Offset-loaded barbell | Same bar, one side 2–5% heavier | Acute EMG and one 4-week ultrasound study only. I would not use it for OHP: it is a technical lift in which a tilted bar is the very thing being examined |

### 4b. Mobility restriction confirmed

I did not find controlled evidence on mobility work that restores overhead range in lifters specifically; the evidence I opened on scapular-focused exercise is in symptomatic or dyskinetic athletes (Section 6). The convention is 5 minutes daily of the specific limit (wall slides for flexion, pec minor stretch, thoracic extension over a bench), re-measured at 4 weeks with the same inclinometer. **Evidence: tier 6 plus inference. Confidence: low.** Anything that does not move the number in a block has not worked, and a restriction with a hard stop at one shoulder goes to Section 7.

### 4c. Control or habit

A single constant cue (bar level, a visible target, a mirror or a live front camera on the first set) and a re-film. Feedback in general improves lifting output and technical competency (Weakley 2023, 20 studies, abstract; population mixed), but no study tests it on this fault. **Confidence: low.**

## 5. The barbell OHP itself

**Keep the barbell OHP on the heavy day. Do not replace it.**

1. It is a named strength goal, it is trained to a block reference, and the benefit of a heavy bilateral probe cannot be had from a dumbbell variant. Kassiano 2025 gives the specific reason: bilateral strength follows bilateral training.
2. The cost of swapping the heavy-day lift is real, in the engine and in the training. The reference is built from the heavy-day rows (`blockReference`, `src/core/progression.js`), and history is scoped by `dayKey` (CLAUDE.md). A new exercise id on Sunday would start with no reference, and `historyAliasDayKey` does not help with exercise substitution. The reference is also monotone by design (decision of 2026-09-30), so there is no path to bring a lower number back in.
3. The same engine facts make the Wednesday OHP (day A, `double_progression`, 3 x 6-8 at RPE <= 8, `src/program/program.v3.js` lines 128) the safe place to substitute. Its history is its own and does not feed the heavy-day reference. Replacing it with a single-arm press starts a new history, so the first Wednesday is a cold start (load by feel, one session of calibration), and the barbell volume-day history stops accumulating. That is a cheap cost.
4. **Substitute, don't add.** 3 sets bilateral become 3 sets per side. Each arm still gets 3 sets, so the fractional count for front delt, triceps and upper pec is unchanged (SYNTHESIS 2.1). The displaced work is the second barbell exposure of the week, not any accessory. Session time rises by roughly 8–10 minutes (six working sets with alternating sides).
5. **Logging without per-side entries.** Log only the weaker side's reps and load. The stronger side matches the same reps. This gives the engine a limiting-side number and the athlete never enters a per-side set. The cost is that the stronger side is held to the weaker side's performance, which for a gap under ~10% is a small dose reduction; I flag it as convention.
6. **Trigger and gate.** Substitute only if Section 2 finds a replicated strength or control deficit. If it does not, the barbell stays on both days and nothing changes. Revert after the 4-week gate if the re-test shows no improvement; a substitution that does nothing is a cost.
7. The one engine-side snag: a single-arm dumbbell press needs either a `custom:` exercise or a new catalogue id, and I have not checked whether the weighted-reps logger handles "per side" annotation. That is an implementation matter for the lead.

**The alternative I considered:** keep both barbell sessions and add single-arm work beside them. It fails on the project's own rule. It adds front-delt/triceps volume to a week where SYNTHESIS 2.1 holds delts and triceps flat at the top of the effective band, and it has no evidence that the addition does anything the substitution would not.

## 6. The trap component

**Finding: the usual blame (upper-trap dominance, scapular upward-rotation asymmetry) is supported by the field's teaching and thinly supported by evidence, and in a trained pressing lifter it is untestable at home with any validity.**

| Claim | Evidence | Population | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scapular dyskinesis is common in healthy people | Prevalence 61% overhead vs 33% non-overhead athletes (Burn 2016, systematic review, 12 studies, n = 1,401, abstract). Scapular asymmetry equally prevalent in asymptomatic (71–77%) and symptomatic (71–76%) athletes (search summary of one study, unverified primary) | Athletes, mostly overhead | Moderate that it is common; the point is that it does not discriminate |
| Visual rating is unreliable | κ 0.48–0.61 (McClure 2009, n = 142, expert raters, standard protocol); κ 0.40–0.44 (Uhl 2009, n = 56); κ 0.12, with belief affecting ratings (Vila-Dieguez 2026, 104 physiotherapists, healthy videos); no qualitative method reliable (Paraskevopoulos 2020, 14 studies); yes/no screen poorly accurate against sEMG (Ramiscal 2025, n = 72 asymptomatic) | Mixed | High that visual rating is unreliable, especially outside trained raters |
| What the visual rating tracks | Shoulders judged dyskinetic had less upward rotation and clavicular elevation on 3D (Tate 2009, n = 66 of 142; abstract) but dyskinesis was unrelated to symptoms | Collegiate overhead athletes | Moderate that something real is being seen; it is just not pathology |
| It predicts pain | +43% relative risk of shoulder pain, 5 prospective studies, n = 419 athletes, 35% vs 25% over 9–24 months (Hickey 2018). No higher prevalence in 67 shoulder-pain patients vs 68 controls (Plummer 2017). No association with pain in n = 45 professional overhead athletes (Cozzolino 2026, abstract) | Athletes (mostly throwers, swimmers); not barbell lifters | Low-moderate; conflicting, the pooled estimate is small and from five studies |
| Upper trap overactive, serratus underactive | 22-study EMG review: "for the majority of muscles, regardless of task, load or arm position, significant differences were not demonstrated or results were contradictory"; limited evidence for serratus anterior lower amplitude, delayed activation, earlier termination (Kinsella and Pizzari 2017, abstract). Upper trap overactivity not established | Subacromial pain syndrome vs controls | Moderate that the upper-trap story is unsupported; low-moderate for serratus |
| Scapular exercise corrects it | 20 studies, "conflicting evidence" on scapular position and motion; exercise reduces pain and disability in impingement (Nodehi Moghadam 2020, abstract). 14 studies, n = 412, mean age 23.8: moderate-certainty improvement in function, disability, range over 6–12 weeks; pain, strength and sport outcomes low certainty (Solana-Tramunt 2026, abstract) | Symptomatic or dyskinetic athletes | Low for his case; shoulder function and range may improve, strength and performance not shown |

**Kibler's 2013 "scapular summit" consensus, which the field cites, I could only see through search summaries** (dyskinesis described as an impairment rather than a diagnosis, altering efficiency and joint mechanics). Treat as expert consensus (tier 5), not evidence.

**What would distinguish a trap/scapular problem from a deltoid strength difference:**

- Deltoid or pressing-muscle strength difference: scales with load and failure proximity; present in isolation (single-arm press, lateral raise at a fixed load, reps differ on two days); not changed by assisting the scapula.
- Scapular control: present unloaded or with a 1–2 kg weight; passive range intact; changes with cueing or a manual scapular assist; may be visible as winging or early shrug at elevation. Its reliability as a visual call is low, so the call should rest on whether the difference **changes with the assist**, not on the look alone.
- Trap size or resting height: a different question again. A resting shoulder-height difference of several mm is normal in a healthy adolescent cohort (mean 7.5 +/- 5.8 mm, range 0-27, one study, search summary) and scoliosis or a head tilt will create a trapezius contour difference without any pressing deficit. Row 5 of the differential covers that.

**Does the answer change the programming? Barely.** The programme already contains the evidence-adjacent exercises for a strong shoulder girdle (face pulls, rear-delt work, pull-ups, rows, laterals). A scapular-specific block has weak evidence for changing performance, and the diagnosis matters mainly for **referral** (winging, atrophy) and for knowing whether the single-arm substitution in Section 5 is the right tool. I would not add scapular drills as an additional load.

## 7. Red flags

I am not a clinician. The list below is built from sources I could open (StatPearls on scapular winging, full page) and from clinical-guideline summaries I only saw as search results (Alberta Health Services shoulder pathways, a JOSPT 2025 rotator-cuff guideline I could not open, a medRxiv red-flag scoping protocol). Some items are general medical knowledge and I mark them. **If any of these is true, stop pressing and get assessed by a physiotherapist or sports physician. If you are unsure, over-refer.**

**Pain and symptoms**
- Pain during or after pressing that is sharp, pinching, or at a repeatable arc; pain at rest or at night that is not position-dependent; pain that is getting worse over weeks despite lighter loads.
- Pain, tingling, numbness or weakness travelling below the elbow, into the hand, or with neck movement. (Cervical root or nerve involvement; general medical knowledge.)
- Catching, clicking with pain, locking, or a feeling that the shoulder will slip or give way (apprehension).
- Arm swelling, heaviness, colour change or a visibly engorged forearm after overhead or pressing work. (Possible venous thoracic-outlet problem; general medical knowledge.)
- Fever, unexplained weight loss, cancer history, night pain that nothing eases. (Serious pathology; named in the guideline summaries.)

**Findings**
- A **lost range** rather than a weak one: a firm, unyielding stop on one side that warm-up, stretching and a week of work do not shift, especially if it is a new change.
- **Winging of the shoulder blade at rest or with a wall push**, or a shoulder that droops. In StatPearls (full page): serratus anterior palsy, hard to lift the arm above 120 degrees, winging with forward push; trapezius palsy, drooping shoulder, winging with abduction. Weightlifting is a listed cause of long thoracic nerve injury. Diagnosis needs examination and electrodiagnostics.
- **Visible muscle wasting** in the upper trapezius, supraspinatus/infraspinatus fossa or the deltoid, or a change in trapezius contour that was not there before.
- **Sudden loss of strength** on one side, or a side that cannot complete a lift it did last week.
- History of a dislocation, fall, clavicle or AC-joint injury, neck or shoulder surgery, or a lymph-node biopsy in the neck (the commonest cause of spinal accessory nerve injury per StatPearls).
- Positive forward-bend test (rib hump) or a clear lateral curve, before spending months chasing it with pressing variants. (Adams test evidence is adolescent screening; flag as transfer.)

**What does not need a clinician:** a painless, long-standing, load-independent or load-dependent bar-path difference that is the same size as the take-to-take spread and does not progress. That is a coaching question.

## Where the intuitive answer holds, and where it fails

| Intuition | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "He has an imbalance." | Plausible and probably small; not yet established. Two weak and non-independent observations do not make a measurement. |
| "It's the traps/scapulae." | The explanation is popular and thinly supported; visual rating is unreliable. |
| "Add unilateral work." | Addition conflicts with flat-volume decisions and has no evidence of benefit; substitution on the volume day is the defensible form, and only if a deficit is replicated. |
| "Train the weak side first and match down." | Convention. Cheap, harmless, unproven. |
| "Replace the barbell with dumbbells for a while." | Poor trade. Bilateral strength follows bilateral practice; the heavy-day reference and goal are the barbell lift. |
| "Fix it before it causes an injury." | Injury prediction from asymmetry is inconsistent even in the lower-limb literature it comes from; there is no upper-limb pressing evidence. |

## Recommendations for this athlete

1. **First, no programme change.** Re-film per Section 2a (empty bar, ~55%, last heavy back-off; front and rear; bar level; second take for noise). Check grip marks. Do not look at one side before the film is done.
2. **Then, once, on the rest day:** the two-day single-arm seated dumbbell test (Section 2b) and the wall-flexion and pec-minor checks (Section 2c). Total about 40 minutes across two days.
3. **Decide by the table in 2e.** If nothing clears the noise and nothing hurts, stop, and re-film once per block.
4. **If a strength deficit replicates:** replace Wednesday's barbell OHP (3 x 6-8) with seated single-arm dumbbell press 3 x 6-8 per side, weaker side's reps are the logged number and the stronger side matches. Keep the Sunday barbell probe and back-offs untouched. Review at 4 weeks and 8-10 weeks.
5. **If a mobility restriction replicates:** five minutes of daily targeted work and re-measure at 4 weeks. No programme change to the lifts.
6. **If it is habit or control:** one cue, re-film, no new exercises.
7. **Any red flag: refer.**
8. **Cost-free micro-adjustment regardless:** on single-arm accessories that already exist (cable lateral raise on Wednesday and Sunday), pick the load for the weaker-feeling side, do equal reps on both, and begin with the side you like less. This is convention, takes no extra time, adds no sets and needs no per-side logging.

## Conflicts, uncertainties and open questions

- **Is any of the 3-part chain (real, strength, fixable) true for him?** I cannot say; the footage and tests do not exist yet.
- **My ~8-11% noise figure is arithmetic on a barbell-1RM CV**, applied to a dumbbell rep test whose CV is unpublished. If his own two-day spread turns out to be 1–2 reps, the 2-rep rule is too lax; if it is 3 reps, too strict.
- **The offset-loading hypertrophy papers** came through search summaries, authors unconfirmed, and may overlap. The pec thickness figures (0.20 vs 0.13 cm over 4 weeks) are tiny and near the ultrasound error floor. Treat as hypothesis-generating.
- **I found no published study at all** of side-to-side bar tilt, lockout, or lag in the barbell OHP in trained lifters, nor of handedness effects on OHP. The absence is itself the finding.
- **Cross-education and "weak side first"** are repeated in coaching writing with minimal support (Wong 2024: 3 eligible studies).
- **Hickey 2018's +43%** is the only prospective support for dyskinesis mattering, and other recent work (Cozzolino 2026, Witten 2026) finds no association with pain or outcome; I leave the conflict unresolved.
- **Would change my recommendation:** pain or any Section 7 item (refer); a replicated strength deficit above ~10% (supports substitution); a restriction that persists after 4 weeks (refer); an engine constraint that forbids a one-for-one swap on the volume day (then do nothing structural and rely on cueing).
- **Not covered:** hypertrophy balance of the shoulders and traps (hypertrophy-coach's remit, and there is no valid home measurement), leg training (the Section 1 row 6 test needs only a seated press), and running interactions (running-coach).
